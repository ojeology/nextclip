Pricing is the first business skill a writer needs, and the one nobody teaches. There is no universal rate — but there is a method for finding yours, and numbers from the current market to sanity-check it against.

**The three ways to price.** Per word is the industry shorthand and the easiest to quote. Per hour suits messy, open-ended work where scope is genuinely unpredictable. A flat fee for the whole piece — research, drafts and revisions included — is usually the most profitable, because it lets your speed and skill be the profit margin instead of a pay cut.

**The market bands, roughly.** Beginners commonly start around $0.05–$0.15 per word; intermediate writers land in the $0.10–$0.30 band; experienced specialists command $0.25–$0.60 and beyond, with technical, finance and medical writing at the top of the range. Hourly, solid commercial work tends to run $30–$60 early on and $50–$100+ with a track record. Treat these as orientation, not permission — niche and negotiating position move every number.

**Know your floor before you look at anyone's rates.** Your floor is the least you can accept per hour without losing money: your target income, plus business expenses, plus tax, divided by the hours you can realistically bill. A writer targeting $24,000 a year with $1,200 of expenses, a 25% tax set-aside and about 20 billable hours a week needs roughly $35 an hour — anything below that is a subsidy you are paying to the client.

**The per-word trap.** Per-word pricing quietly punishes you for getting faster and better. At 500 words an hour, $0.10 a word is $50 an hour; at the same speed, $0.25 a word is $125. The same article, the same effort — a fivefold difference in what your hour is worth. This is why experienced writers convert every per-word offer into an effective hourly number before accepting.

**The flat-fee advantage.** Quote the piece, not the word count. A flat fee rewards research efficiency, discourages padded word counts, and gives the client certainty. Scope it in one line: topic, length, one revision round, delivery date. Anything outside that line is a new quote.

**Say the number.** Pricing fails most often at the moment of speech. Practice one sentence until it is boring: "For a piece like that, my fee is X." No apology, no discount before they ask, no range unless they ask for one.

**When to raise.** After every few completed projects, nudge the number for new clients — existing ones move to the new rate on renewal, not mid-project. The writers who earn well are rarely the best writers; they are the ones who raised rates before fear finished its sentence.
