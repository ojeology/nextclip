Most apologies fail in the same place. They contain the word sorry and no acceptance of responsibility, so the person receiving them feels managed rather than heard — and being managed when you expected an apology is worse than getting nothing.

The structure below works for a personal apology and for an organisational one, because the psychology is identical.

## The six parts

1. **Say sorry, plainly and early.** "I'm sorry." Not "I want to apologise", which is a statement about your intentions rather than an apology.
2. **Name what you did.** Specifically. This is the part that proves you understand, and it is the part evasive apologies skip. "I'm sorry I read your messages" is an apology. "I'm sorry about what happened" is not.
3. **Acknowledge the effect.** Say what it cost them. Not what you meant — what they experienced.
4. **No excuses in the same breath.** Explanation and justification are almost indistinguishable to someone who is hurt. If context genuinely matters, it goes later and briefly, introduced as explanation and explicitly not as defence.
5. **Say what you will do.** The repair, and the change that stops it recurring. An apology with no behaviour attached is a wish.
6. **Do not demand forgiveness.** "I hope you can forgive me" places a task on them. Better: "You don't have to reply to this."

## The phrases that destroy an apology

- **"I'm sorry if…"** — conditional. It suggests the harm may not have happened.
- **"I'm sorry you feel…"** — this apologises for their reaction, not your action. It is the most resented construction in the language.
- **"I'm sorry, but…"** — everything before "but" is deleted by everything after it.
- **"Mistakes were made."** — the passive with no actor. Famous precisely because it is evasive.
- **"I'm sorry it came across that way."** — relocates the fault to their interpretation.
- **"I was just…"** — minimising.

## Organisational apologies

The same rules, plus three:

**Speed matters.** A late apology is read as a response to pressure rather than to the harm. If you do not yet know the facts, say what you know, say what you do not, say when you will update, and then actually update.

**Name the harm without hiding behind process language.** "We regret any inconvenience caused by the service interruption" is not an apology for losing three days of customer data. Say what happened in words a customer would use.

**Say what changes.** Specific, verifiable, with a date. Anything else reads as a press release.

Legal advice will often push towards vagueness. That instinct is worth weighing carefully: in many places, apologising is not in itself an admission of liability, and the reputational cost of an evasive statement usually exceeds the legal risk of a clear one. Take advice on your own jurisdiction — but do not let the drafting default to meaninglessness without asking.

## Length and medium

Short. A personal apology is three to six sentences; adding paragraphs usually means you have started explaining yourself.

Match the medium to the harm. Something serious deserves a conversation or a letter, not a message. Something small does not need a summit — an over-elaborate apology for a minor thing makes the other person manage your feelings.

## Example

> Amara,
>
> I'm sorry I missed the deadline on the client report and didn't tell you until the morning it was due. That left you presenting without the figures and having to explain a gap that wasn't yours.
>
> I should have flagged it on the Wednesday when I knew I was behind. I've moved my check-in to Monday so I catch this earlier, and I've put the outstanding sections into the shared folder so you can see the state of them.
>
> You don't need to reply to this.

## Common mistakes

Apologising for the reaction rather than the act. Explaining before apologising. Making it long, so the recipient has to reassure you. Apologising repeatedly for the same thing, which becomes its own burden. Promising a change you will not make. Apologising in public for something private, or in private for something public.

## Practical tips

- Write it, then delete every sentence explaining why you did it. Read what remains — it is usually the actual apology.
- Search your draft for "if", "but" and "you feel". Each one is a probable failure.
- Say what you will do differently in one concrete sentence.
- Then do it. The apology is only the first half.
