A thesis or dissertation is the longest piece of writing most people will ever do — and it can feel overwhelming. The way through it is not a burst of genius but slow, steady, structured work: one chapter, one section, one paragraph at a time.

## What it is

A thesis is a long, original piece of research submitted for a degree. It argues a position, supported by evidence you gathered or analysed, and it follows a standard academic structure.

## How to write it

**Start with a clear research question.** A thesis has a spine: one question you set out to answer. Define it early, and let everything you write serve it.

**Break it into chapters, then sections, then paragraphs.** A thesis is built by assembling small pieces. Outline your chapters and sections before you write, and work through them one at a time. The outline generator helps here.

**Write badly, then fix it.** Do not wait to write the perfect sentence. Get a rough draft down — even a messy one — because a draft you can edit is far better than no draft. You will rewrite it anyway.

**Write the introduction last.** The introduction summarises what you found and argues. It is much easier once the research and the results are done.

**Keep the same structure as a research paper** — abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, conclusion — but at greater length and depth.

**Reference as you go.** Record each source as you use it, in the required style. Reconstructing references at the end is painful and error-prone.

**Keep a regular schedule.** A thesis is a marathon. Writing a little every day, even a half hour, beats waiting for big blocks of free time.

## How to manage the length

**Don't try to do it all at once.** Focus on one section. "This week I am writing the methods chapter" is a real goal; "write my thesis" is not.

**Accept the messiness of a first draft.** Perfectionism is the enemy of progress on a long project. Write, then revise.

**Seek feedback early and often.** Show your supervisor sections as you go, not the whole thing at the end.

## Common mistakes

Waiting for inspiration. Trying to perfect each paragraph before moving on. No outline — writing without a plan, then getting lost. Leaving citations for later. A vague research question. Being so afraid of imperfection that you do not write.

## Practical tips

- Define one small task per writing session.
- Write the introduction and abstract last.
- Use your supervisor early, not late.
