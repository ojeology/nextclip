The UK market is smaller than the US and more relationship-driven. Fewer desks, fewer commissions, and a stronger convention that you pitch an idea rather than submit a finished piece. The upside is that a good pitch reaches a real person more often than it does in the US.

## The word "commissioning"

British publications talk about **commissioning editors**, and the word tells you how the market works. You pitch; if they like it, they commission the piece and you write it to an agreed brief, length and fee. Sending a completed 2,000-word feature unsolicited is unusual outside literary magazines and is often read as not understanding the market.

Find the commissioning editor for the specific section. The masthead lists them.

## The pitch

**Subject: a plain description of the idea.** UK desks are less standardised on the "PITCH:" prefix than US ones, and either is fine.

**Two or three paragraphs.** The idea, why now, and why you.

**Slightly more context is tolerated than in the US** — but not much. The first sentence still has to carry the idea.

**Say the length and when you can file.**

Under 300 words.

## Rates and the per-1,000 convention

UK rates are conventionally quoted **per 1,000 words**, which trips up writers used to per-word figures. £250 per 1,000 words is 25p a word — the same number expressed differently, and it is worth converting before you decide whether an offer is good.

Ask three things before filing:

- **The fee, in writing.** "We pay standard rates" is not a fee.
- **When payment is made** — on publication or on acceptance. On acceptance is better and some titles do it.
- **Whether there is a kill fee** if the piece is dropped.

Late payment is a widespread problem in UK freelance journalism. Invoice promptly, keep the correspondence, and chase without embarrassment — it is expected, not rude.

## Style conventions to get right

Set your spellchecker to UK English before you file. Beyond `-ise` versus `-ize`, watch:

- **Punctuation outside quotation marks** unless it belongs to the quotation
- **Single quotes** in many house styles
- **No Oxford comma** in most UK publications
- **Collective nouns take plural verbs** — "the government are considering"
- **Titles without full stops** — Mr, Dr, Ms

A copy-editor will fix these, but a pitch written in obvious US style signals you have not read the publication.

## Following up

A fortnight, once, politely, in the same thread. UK desks are often smaller and slower than US ones, and a chase after four days lands badly.

## Common mistakes

Sending a finished feature nobody commissioned. Pitching the editor-in-chief rather than the section's commissioning editor. Not naming a fee expectation when asked. US spelling throughout. Assuming a per-word rate when the offer is per 1,000. Not invoicing promptly, then finding the fee has drifted three months.

## Practical tips

- Read the section, not the front page, and name a specific slot if the publication has recurring ones.
- Ask for the fee in writing before you start. This is normal and nobody will think less of you.
