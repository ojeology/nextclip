**BRYME has not run these tools as a paying customer over months, so this is not a ranked "best" list.** It is the one decision that determines whether a note app serves a writing career for a decade or becomes a data-hostage situation, plus the shortlist and the tests.

Note-taking is the category where writers over-shop. The honest starting observation: **almost any of these tools works**. Apple Notes, free, handles capture for millions of people. The differences that matter are narrower than the comparison tables imply — and one of them is nearly irreversible.

## The ownership question

When you write things down for a living, your notes become an asset: interview archives, half-formed essay ideas, a decade of thinking. Two architectures exist:

- **Plain files on your disk** (Markdown or similar): Obsidian, Logseq, Joplin, any text editor. If the company vanishes tomorrow, your notes are untouched. Sync is an add-on, not the product.
- **A cloud database** the app owns: Notion, Evernote, and most modern entrants. Convenient, collaborative, and searchable — but export is a courtesy, not a right, and it is usually lossy. Links between notes, databases and formatting rarely survive the journey out.

The cautionary tale is Evernote — a decade the default note app, then repeated price rises and a capped free plan; entry paid tiers now run roughly **$8–15/month** depending on tier, for features that were the free baseline in 2015. Nothing was "lost". But thousands of writers learned what a *closed cloud format* costs at renegotiation time.

**The rule this site would offer: write in formats you could open without the vendor.** If that constraint is met, everything else is preference.

## The shortlist, mapped to how you work

| You are… | Reasonable pick | The cost |
|---|---|---|
| An Apple writer who wants beauty and speed | **Bear** — Markdown, elegant, fast | Free single-device; Pro ~$3/month ($29.99/yr) for sync and export; Apple-only |
| Building a long-term knowledge base | **Obsidian** — plain Markdown files, backlinks, huge plugin ecosystem | Free locally; Sync ~$4–8/month; commercial use ~$50/yr |
| A thinker in outlines and daily journals | **Logseq** — outliner-first, open source, local files | Free; official sync ~$5/month |
| Running client work, wikis, databases with collaborators | **Notion** — genuinely unmatched as a team workspace | Free personal; Plus ~$10–12/user/month; AI add-on extra |
| Inside Microsoft/Apple ecosystems already | **OneNote / Apple Notes** — zero friction, already paid for | Free |
| An open-source maximalist | **Joplin** — Evernote-shaped, self-hostable, encrypted | Free; cloud sync from ~$3–4/month |

The rest — Roam, Craft, Reflect, Tana, Capacities — are variations with real merits and smaller margins for error. None of them changes the rule above.

## The trap: note-taking as procrastination

Writers move their notes between apps the way other people reorganise a desk. The symptom is building a *system* — tags, databases, dashboards — instead of producing drafts. Two structural defences:

1. **One inbox, reviewed weekly.** Every idea lands in one place; processing is a scheduled habit, not a background anxiety. (If the ideas are the problem rather than the storage, the fix is the [brainstorming discipline](/learn/how-to-brainstorm-and-find-ideas/), not new software.)
2. **Notes end in drafts or they are compost.** A note that never becomes a pitch, an essay or a book section has a role — but a note app that only ever accumulates is a diary with a subscription fee. Journaling writers should be honest that that is what they are doing; the [journaling guides](/learn/journaling-personal/how-to-start-journaling/) serve that purpose without a PKM learning curve.

## The three tests to run

1. **The outage test.** Turn on flight mode and open yesterday's notes. If they are not there, you have rented your thinking.
2. **The export test.** Export everything today, in the format you would need in an emergency. Look at what survives. Do this before the library gets big, and annually after.
3. **The capture test.** Next flash of an idea — on a train, mid-conversation — time how many seconds to get it into the app. Capture friction is the only spec that predicts daily use.

## What to do this month

1. Pick by architecture first (files vs cloud), then by table above — and resist migrating anything until the new tool has held a month of real use.
2. Run the three tests on your current setup. If your present tool passes, the search is over; spend the energy on writing.
3. If starting fresh: plain Markdown locally, free, no account. It is the only choice that can never be wrong.
4. Keep the [long-form writing software](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/choosing-long-form-writing-software/) decision separate — thinking tools and drafting tools have different jobs, and forcing one app to do both is how the procrastination trap opens.
