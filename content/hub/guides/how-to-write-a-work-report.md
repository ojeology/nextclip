A workplace report answers a question for a reader who is busy. The best ones put the answer up top, present the evidence clearly, and make the recommendation easy to act on.

## What it is

A work report is a brief, factual summary of a situation, project, or set of findings, written for a manager, client, or team. It is built to inform a decision.

## How to write it

**Lead with the answer.** Put the key finding or recommendation first, in an executive summary. A busy manager reads that and decides whether to go deeper.

**Define the purpose and reader.** What decision is this informing? Who reads it? State the scope so the reader knows what is and is not covered.

**Use plain, clear language.** Avoid corporate jargon and long, roundabout sentences. Short paragraphs, clear headings, and a logical flow.

**Present facts objectively.** Let the evidence speak. Use headings and tables for data; keep opinion clearly labelled as recommendation, not fact.

**Structure it.** Typical sections: purpose, summary, findings, analysis, recommendation, next steps. Keep data in tables where it helps.

**Give a clear recommendation.** A report that finds a problem but suggests no way forward is incomplete. Say what you recommend and why.

**Keep it as short as the job needs.** Do not pad. Extra length buries the point and wastes the reader's time.

## Common mistakes

Burying the finding at the bottom. Jargon and passive voice. Long blocks of text. No recommendation. Reporting everything with no analysis. Awkwardly long without a clear structure.

## Practical tips

- Write the summary last, once you know the findings.
- Read it as a busy person and cut whatever they would skip.
- End with a concrete next step.

## Checklist

- [ ] I state the purpose and reader up front.
- [ ] My summary leads with the key finding and recommendation.
- [ ] I used plain language and clear headings.
- [ ] I present facts objectively.
- [ ] I made a clear recommendation.
- [ ] I kept it as short as the job needs.
