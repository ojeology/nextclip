Academic writing is a particular, often intimidating style. The good news is that it has a clear structure and clear rules — and none of it requires you to be dishonest.

## What it is

Academic writing is formal prose used to present an argument or report findings to a scholarly reader. It is precise, evidence-based, and well-referenced, and it is meant to be *your* work built on sources — never a copy of them.

## Why it is used

It is how knowledge is shared and tested: you make a claim and support it with evidence. The goal is not to sound clever; it is to be clear, accurate and honest.

## When to use it

Essays, research papers, literature reviews, reports, dissertations, and theses.

## Typical structure

1. **Introduction** — the topic, the question, and what you will argue.
2. **Body** — the supporting argument or analysis, in ordered sections.
3. **Conclusion** — what you have established and what it means.

For a research paper, the sections are often: abstract, introduction, methodology, results, discussion, conclusion, references.

## How to write it

**Understand the assignment.** What is the question, what is the required length, and what style of citation (APA, MLA, Harvard, etc.)?

**Answer the question you are given.** Do not write the essay you wanted to write; answer the one set.

**Build a clear argument, not a summary.** You are not stringing together facts; you are making a point and defending it.

**Support every claim with evidence.** Reference sources for facts and ideas that are not your own or common knowledge. Cite as you go, not at the end.

**Use an academic tone.** Third person where expected, precise and measured language, and no slang or unsupported claims.

**Do your own thinking.** The sources are tools that support your argument, not a substitute for it.

**Write honestly.** Never copy a passage, never turn in work you have not written, and never present another's ideas as your own.

## Common mistakes

Answering a different question than the one set. Writing a summary instead of an argument. Plagiarising through poor paraphrasing or missing citations. Using sources to fill space rather than support a point. Over-using fancy vocabulary to sound academic.

## Practical tips

- Write an outline that directly answers the question before you draft.
- Keep your thesis clear and repeat it in the conclusion.
- Cite sources the moment you use them to avoid losing them.
- Ask: does every paragraph support my argument and my question?

## Checklist

- [ ] I am answering the exact question set.
- [ ] I have a clear thesis or argument.
- [ ] Every claim I did not generate is cited.
- [ ] I used the required citation style correctly.
- [ ] I wrote in an appropriate academic tone.
- [ ] The work is genuinely my own analysis.
