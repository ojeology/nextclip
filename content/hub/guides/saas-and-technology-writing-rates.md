SaaS and technology content has a reputation as an easy high-paying niche. The rate data is more complicated than that reputation suggests, and the complication is worth understanding before you specialise.

## The rates

From UK market analysis built on the **ProCopywriters 2025 survey** (474 freelance copywriters, average day rate £480):

| Specialism | UK day rate |
|---|---|
| Technology and software copywriting | £450–£750 |
| Financial services and fintech | £600–£850 |
| Overall UK average (all copywriting) | £480 |
| Top 10% of UK freelancers (all disciplines) | ~£708 |

From the **Editorial Freelancers Association 2026 Rate Chart** (1,100+ respondents), the closest US categories:

| Category | Per word | Per hour |
|---|---|---|
| Technical writing | 87.5¢–$1.08 | $75–$126 |
| Business/marketing (ghostwriting) | 32¢–87.5¢ | $65–$75 |
| Blog posts | 25¢–45¢ | $74.50–$87.50 |
| Articles and essays | 25¢–45¢ | $70–$100 |

Software and SaaS documentation specifically is reported at $60–$130/hour in 2026 market analyses, with API and developer documentation higher at $80–$175.

## The catch nobody mentions

Technology copywriting sits **below** financial services in every UK breakdown, despite tech having the louder reputation for paying well. There is a structural reason:

> **Many SaaS companies are startups, and startups have small budgets.**

A pre-Series-A company may genuinely want good writing and genuinely be unable to pay £700 a day for it. Meanwhile an insurer with a compliance department has both the need and the budget.

This means the SaaS "niche" is really two different markets wearing one label:

**Enterprise and scale-up B2B** — real content budgets, established marketing teams, rates at the top of the range. Often the work is technical: integration guides, security documentation, migration content.

**Early-stage startups** — enthusiasm, equity offers, and rates at or below general content. Frequently a poor use of a specialist's time, however interesting the product.

Choosing between them is the single biggest determinant of what you earn in this niche.

## Where the money actually is

Ranked by what they typically pay:

1. **Developer-facing content** — API documentation, integration guides, technical tutorials. $80–$175/hour. Smallest supply of competent writers.
2. **Security and compliance content** — SOC 2, ISO, data protection explainers. $85–$160/hour. Growing quickly.
3. **Technical white papers and research reports** — priced as marketing assets, often the highest per-hour work available.
4. **Case studies** — consistently well paid because they require interviewing customers and most writers avoid the coordination.
5. **Product and lifecycle email** — onboarding sequences, feature launches. Undervalued relative to its revenue impact.
6. **SEO blog content** — the most abundant and the worst paid. This is the part of the market AI has compressed hardest.

Notice that the ordering tracks technical depth almost exactly. The further you sit from "write a 1,500-word blog post about productivity", the better the rate.

## Pricing against a startup

Three approaches that work when the budget is genuinely constrained:

**Scope down, don't rate down.** Do fewer deliverables at your rate rather than more at a discount. A discounted rate is very difficult to reverse with the same client.

**Price the outcome where you can measure it.** A case study that closes deals, or documentation that reduces support tickets, has a business case. Blog volume rarely does.

**Retainers for predictability, at a stated discount.** A defined monthly scope at 10–15% below your project rate is a fair trade for guaranteed income. An open-ended "few pieces a month" is not.

## Breaking in

**Pick a category, not "tech".** Developer tools, fintech infrastructure, healthtech, cybersecurity — each has its own vocabulary and buyer. "I write for SaaS" says nothing.

**Use the product before you write about it.** Most free tiers exist. Writing from a press release rather than experience is instantly visible to a technical reader.

**Publish one deeply technical piece.** One genuinely expert article beats a portfolio of generic marketing content, because the client's actual question is whether you understand the subject.

**Learn the funnel vocabulary.** Knowing the difference between top-of-funnel education and bottom-of-funnel comparison content lets you talk about business impact instead of word count — which is how you get out of per-word pricing.
