Spelling differences are well known. Punctuation, date and number conventions are less discussed and cause more actual confusion, because getting a date wrong is not a style slip — it is a factual error.

## Dates: the one that genuinely misleads

This is the most important item in this guide.

- **UK, Australia, Ireland, most of the Commonwealth:** day-month-year. `06/09/2026` means **6 September 2026**.
- **United States:** month-day-year. `06/09/2026` means **June 9, 2026**.
- **Canada:** genuinely mixed. Both are in use, which makes it the most dangerous market of all for numeric dates.

Those two readings of `06/09/2026` are **three months apart**. In a piece with a deadline, an event date or a filing date, that is not a style preference — it is wrong information.

**The fix is simple and universal: spell the month out.** `6 September 2026` or `September 6, 2026` cannot be misread by anyone. Use numeric dates only when a publication's style demands it.

Note the comma, too. American style puts one after the day in a full date (*September 6, 2026*); British style does not (*6 September 2026*).

## Quotation marks

**American:** double quotes outside, single inside. Commas and full stops go **inside** the closing quote, regardless of logic.

> She called it "a complete disaster," then left.

**British:** varies by publication — many use single outside, double inside, though double-outside is now common. Punctuation goes **outside** unless it belongs to the quoted material.

> She called it 'a complete disaster', then left.

The American rule is mechanical: the comma always goes inside. The British rule is logical: the punctuation goes where it belongs. Editors notice this one immediately because it appears in the first paragraph of almost anything.

## The serial (Oxford) comma

The comma before *and* in a list of three or more.

- **US:** standard in most style guides. Use it.
- **UK:** optional and publication-specific. The Guardian omits it; Oxford University Press, unsurprisingly, uses it.
- **Australia and Canada:** generally follow the omit-unless-needed approach.

Everyone agrees on the exception: use it whenever leaving it out creates ambiguity.

## Numbers, currency and measurement

**Currency symbols** all precede the number: `£50`, `$50`, `A$50`, `C$50`.

Where several dollar currencies appear in one piece, disambiguate on first use — `US$50`, `A$50`, `C$50`. This matters more than it sounds: BRYME's own listings span four dollar currencies, and "$50" alone is not information.

**Thousands and decimals** are the same across all four markets — comma for thousands, point for decimals: `1,250.50`. This diverges in continental Europe, so it matters if you are quoting European sources.

**Measurement** is where the UK is genuinely inconsistent, and pretending otherwise gets you caught. The UK uses miles and pints alongside metric everything else. Australia and Canada are metric with informal imperial survivals (Canadians give height in feet). The US is imperial throughout.

**Billion** now means a thousand million everywhere in English. The old British long-scale billion is dead — do not resurrect it.

## Time

- **US:** 12-hour clock, `9:00 a.m.` or `9:00 AM`
- **UK:** 12-hour common, `9am` or `9.00am`, often with a full stop rather than a colon
- **Australia:** `9am`, 24-hour common in official contexts

If you are writing about a deadline across time zones, name the zone. "Midnight UK time, Wednesday 30 September" is unambiguous. "Midnight on the 30th" is not — and BRYME records that exact phrasing from publications precisely because the good ones spell it out.

## What editors actually notice

Ranked by how quickly it registers:

1. **Quotation-mark punctuation** — appears immediately, and instantly signals which market you write for
2. **Date format** — because it can be factually wrong
3. **Serial comma inconsistency** — using it in one sentence and not the next
4. **Currency without a country prefix** in a piece spanning markets
5. Everything else

## The practical approach

**Check the publication's own pages.** Two minutes reading a live article tells you their quote style, comma habit and date format with total reliability. No style guide beats the evidence.

**Spell out dates and name currencies.** These two habits make you unambiguous in every market simultaneously and cost nothing.

**Be consistent above all.** An editor can globally change single quotes to double in seconds. What they cannot fix cheaply is a piece that switches conventions halfway through, because then they have to read every instance and decide.
