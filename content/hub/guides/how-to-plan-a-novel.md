A novel is 70,000 to 110,000 words for most adult fiction. At 500 words a day — a realistic pace alongside a job — that is roughly six months of drafting and as long again to revise.

The problems of a novel are not the problems of a short story scaled up. They are problems of endurance, of holding a structure in your head over months, and of the middle.

## Planner or discovery writer

Neither is correct, and the argument about it wastes more time than either method.

**Planning more** suits plot-driven work, tight structures, mystery and thriller, anyone who loses momentum when unsure what happens next, and anyone with limited writing time who cannot afford to write 30,000 words that get cut.

**Planning less** suits character-driven and literary work, and writers who find that knowing the ending removes the reason to write.

Most working novelists sit in the middle: they know the ending, the major turns, and roughly nothing else. That is usually the most efficient position — enough structure to prevent drift, enough room to discover.

## A minimum viable plan

Even discovery writers benefit from these five:

1. **Who wants what, and what stops them.** The engine. If you cannot state it in a sentence, the book will sag.
2. **The inciting incident.** What breaks the status quo, and roughly where.
3. **The midpoint.** Something that changes the nature of the problem — often a reversal, a revelation, or the protagonist shifting from reacting to acting. This is the single most useful structural landmark, because it splits the middle in half.
4. **The crisis.** The worst moment, where the protagonist must choose and lose something.
5. **The ending.** Not necessarily the last scene — the resolution of the want.

Add scene-level planning a chapter or three ahead as you write, rather than all at once. Plans made twelve months in advance are usually made by someone who did not yet understand the book.

## The middle

The middle is where novels die. Roughly the 30,000 to 60,000 word range, where the opening energy is gone, the ending is too far away to pull, and the whole thing feels shapeless.

What actually helps:

- **A midpoint that changes the question.** If the second half is the same kind of scene as the first, the middle will sag no matter how well written.
- **Escalation.** Each obstacle costs more than the last. If your protagonist can fail at chapter 14 with no consequence, the scene is not working.
- **A subplot that intersects.** Not a parallel story — one that collides with the main line and complicates it.
- **Cutting.** Very often the sag is not a missing scene but four scenes doing the same job.

## Keeping going

**Write to a schedule, not to inspiration.** Same time, same place, most days. The consistency matters far more than the daily word count.

**Set a small daily target and protect it.** 500 words is 90,000 in six months allowing for missed days. Big targets create a debt that becomes a reason to stop.

**Never end a session at a clean stopping point.** Stop mid-scene, ideally mid-sentence. The next session starts instantly instead of with an hour of re-entry.

**Keep a "later" file.** Every time you notice something to fix, write it there and keep drafting. Going back to fix chapter 3 while drafting chapter 20 is the most reliable way to never finish.

**Keep a story bible** — names, ages, eye colour, dates, who knows what and when. You will contradict yourself by month four, and continuity errors are miserable to unpick later.

**Do not show anyone the first half.** Feedback on an unfinished draft is destabilising and usually addresses problems the second half was going to solve.

## Finishing the draft

Get to the end even if you know it is broken. A finished bad draft is a book with problems; an unfinished good draft is nothing. Many novelists write the last third knowing they will rewrite the first — that is normal and it is much faster than perfecting as you go.

Then leave it alone for at least a month before revising. You cannot see it clearly until you have partly forgotten it.

## Common mistakes

Rewriting chapter 1 for a year. Planning so completely that drafting becomes transcription. Starting the story too early — most first novels can lose their first two chapters. A passive protagonist things happen to. A midpoint that changes nothing. Adding characters to fix a pacing problem. Waiting for a clear run of time that will never arrive.

## Practical tips

- Track word count on a calendar. Seeing the streak is unreasonably effective.
- Write the synopsis before you draft; if you cannot, the shape is not there yet.
- Name files by date, and back up in two places.
- Read novels in your form while drafting, and study where their middles turn.
