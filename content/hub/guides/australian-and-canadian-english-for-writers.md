Most guides treat English as a two-way split. It is not. Australian and Canadian English are distinct standards with their own dictionaries and their own rules about what to borrow from where — and if you are pitching into either market, guessing wrong is visible immediately.

## Canadian English: the genuine hybrid

Canadian English is the most mixed of the major varieties, and the mix is not random.

**Keeps from British:**
- *-our* endings: **colour, honour, favour, labour, neighbour**
- *-re* endings: **centre, theatre, metre, litre**
- Doubled consonants: **travelled, cancelled, labelled**
- *Cheque* for a payment, *grey* over *gray*

**Takes from American:**
- *-ize* over *-ise*: **organize, recognize, realize**
- *-yze*: **analyze, paralyze**
- *Tire*, *curb*, *aluminum*, *program*
- Punctuation inside quotation marks, US-style

So the Canadian standard is roughly: **British vowels, American consonants and verb endings.** *Colour* and *organize* in the same sentence is not an inconsistency in Canada — it is the rule.

The authority is the *Canadian Oxford Dictionary*, and Canadian Press style is the newsroom standard. Broadview — one of the Canadian publications BRYME lists — states outright that it follows Canadian Press style and the Canadian Oxford Dictionary. That is the kind of detail worth reading off a publication's own guidelines before you pitch.

**Dates in Canada are genuinely ambiguous.** Both day-month-year and month-day-year are in circulation. Spell the month out; do not make an editor guess.

## Australian English: closer to British, with its own vocabulary

Australian English follows British spelling more consistently than Canadian does.

**Follows British:**
- *-our*: **colour, honour, labour**
- *-re*: **centre, theatre, metre**
- Doubled consonants: **travelled, modelling**
- *-ise* is strongly preferred over *-ize* — more strongly than in Britain itself, where both are accepted

**Diverges from both:**
- **Program**, not *programme* (unlike the UK)
- **Jail**, not *gaol*
- Labor **only** in "Australian Labor Party" — everywhere else it is *labour*. Getting this wrong in a political piece is an obvious tell.

The authority is the *Macquarie Dictionary*.

**Australian vocabulary is a real barrier if you fake it.** Arvo, servo, bottle-o, ute, esky, doona, thongs (footwear, not underwear), fortnight, tradie, Centrelink. The advice here is the opposite of what you might expect: **do not reach for these unless you are Australian.** Nothing marks an outsider faster than a slightly wrong colloquialism. Write clean standard English and let the editor add the local register.

## Why this matters for pitching

BRYME now lists **17 Canadian** and **10 Australian** paid opportunities alongside 71 US and 21 UK. Several carry eligibility rules that make the local variety non-optional:

- **The New Quarterly** publishes Canadian writers exclusively
- **Geist** prioritises Canadians, permanent residents, or work with a strong Canadian connection
- **Island** accepts submissions from Australia, New Zealand and Australians abroad
- **Propel** is UK and Ireland only

If a publication restricts by nationality, its editors read a great many submissions from writers who have not adapted. Spelling that matches the house standard is the cheapest possible signal that you have.

## The quick reference

| | UK | US | Canada | Australia |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| colour / color | colour | color | **colour** | colour |
| centre / center | centre | center | **centre** | centre |
| organise / organize | either | organize | **organize** | organise |
| analyse / analyze | analyse | analyze | **analyze** | analyse |
| travelled / traveled | travelled | traveled | **travelled** | travelled |
| programme / program | programme | program | program | **program** |
| Dictionary | Oxford | Merriam-Webster | Canadian Oxford | Macquarie |

The bolded Canadian column is the one to study — it is the only variety that splits within a single row-set, and it is the one writers most often get wrong by assuming it is simply American.

## Practical advice

**Set your spellchecker properly.** Both Canadian and Australian English exist as options in every major word processor. Most writers never change it from US default, and it shows.

**Read one full article from the target publication.** Faster and more reliable than any table, including this one.

**Never guess at slang.** Standard English written well is always acceptable. Misused local slang never is.

**Check the publication's stated style.** Where a magazine names its dictionary or style guide — as Broadview does — that is not a suggestion.
