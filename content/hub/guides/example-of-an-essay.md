Reading a full essay is the fastest way to understand how one is put together. Below is a complete argumentative essay on a common topic, then a breakdown of the structure behind each part, so you can copy the shape for your own argument.

## The example essay

**Why Schools Should Teach Financial Literacy**

Most students leave school knowing how to solve for x but not how to read a payslip. They can quote Shakespeare and describe photosynthesis, yet many cannot compare two bank offers, understand a loan, or plan a monthly budget. That is a failure of education, and it is fixable. Schools should teach financial literacy because it prepares students for the most common decisions of adult life, reduces costly mistakes, and closes a gap that currently leaves the least-advantaged worst off.

The first reason is practical: financial decisions are universal and unavoidable. Nearly every adult earns money, pays for housing, and manages a budget. These skills are not abstract extras; they are used weekly. A young person who understands interest, debt, and saving is better placed to rent, borrow, and plan than one who does not. Because these decisions touch everyone, the ability to make them well is not a luxury but a basic life skill.

The second reason is that ignorance is expensive. People who do not understand compound interest tend to borrow at high rates and save too little. They miss the value of starting a retirement fund young, when small amounts compound most powerfully. The gap between those who understand money and those who do not widens over a lifetime, sometimes into a chasm of debt or poverty. Teaching the basics early is the cheapest way to prevent expensive mistakes later.

A third reason concerns fairness. Financial education is disproportionately valuable to students from families with little money to lose. A student whose parents never discussed budgeting has no place to learn it at home; a school that teaches it gives every child the same starting point. To leave this to chance is to reward the children of the already-informed and penalise the rest.

Those who oppose the idea argue that school time is already scarce, and that home is the right place for financial teaching. But many homes do not provide it, and the lessons — saving, budgeting, understanding credit — are precisely the kind of thing that is easier to teach systematically in a classroom than inconsistently at the kitchen table. The question is not whether the skill matters, but where it is best taught.

In conclusion, financial literacy prepares young people for the one thing they will all face: managing money. It prevents costly, lifelong mistakes and it offers the greatest benefit to those who have the least. Adding it to the curriculum is a small change with a large and lasting return.

## Why this works

**It states a clear thesis in the introduction.** The introduction ends with one sentence that names the argument and the three reasons that support it. The reader knows exactly where the essay is going.

**Each body paragraph makes one point.** Paragraphs two, three and four each defend one reason — practical value, the cost of ignorance, and fairness. No paragraph mixes arguments.

**It uses a counter-argument.** Paragraph five states the opposing view ("home is the right place") and answers it. This shows balanced thinking and makes the argument stronger.

**Evidence is concrete.** "Borrow at high rates," "compound interest," "the least-advantaged" — the essay reasons with specifics and examples, not vague assertion.

**The conclusion restates without new arguments.** It returns to the thesis and shows why it matters, without introducing a fourth reason.

## The structure to copy

1. **Introduction** — hook, context, and a one-sentence thesis naming the reasons.
2. **Body paragraph 1** — reason one, explained with specifics.
3. **Body paragraph 2** — reason two.
4. **Body paragraph 3** — reason three.
5. **Counter-argument** — state the opposing view, then answer it.
6. **Conclusion** — restate the thesis and its importance, no new points.

## The formula for the thesis

A strong thesis has two jobs: state your position and preview your reasons. "Schools should teach financial literacy *because* [reason 1], [reason 2], and [reason 3]." One sentence, three promises, and the essay delivers on each.

## Common mistakes

A thesis too vague ("financial literacy is important"). Body paragraphs that each make two points. No counter-argument. A conclusion that introduces new ideas. Evidence with no explanation.

## Checklist

- [ ] My introduction ends with a clear thesis and its reasons.
- [ ] Each body paragraph makes exactly one point.
- [ ] I support each point with specific reasoning.
- [ ] I addressed the opposing view.
- [ ] My conclusion restates without adding new points.
- [ ] The essay reads in a clear order.
