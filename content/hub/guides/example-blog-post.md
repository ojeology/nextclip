A blog post is not an essay — readers skim. This complete example shows the structure that keeps skimmers reading, from headline to next step.

## The example blog post

**How to Stop Wasting the First Hour of Your Writing Day**

*Do you open your document, stare at it, and end up scrolling? The problem is not your willpower. It's your first move.*

Every writer has lost the start of a session to the blank page. You sit down, the cursor blinks, and instead of writing you check your phone, tidy your desk, or open a tab you did not need. Twenty minutes gone.

The fix is not motivation. It is a first move so small it is impossible to refuse.

**Start with a single sentence, not a session.**

Do not tell yourself "I am going to write for an hour." Tell yourself "I am going to write one sentence about the part I understand best." A one-line goal removes the fear of a big task.

Your goal here is not good writing. It is to start. Once the cursor is moving, stopping becomes harder than continuing.

**Write the section you can already write.**

If your introduction is hard, skip it. Most writers have one section in their head — a story, an example, a point they know well. Start there. You can order the piece later.

**Use a timer to lower the bar.**

Set ten minutes. Write without stopping to judge or fix anything. When the timer rings, decide whether to continue. Most people do. The timer turns "be good" into "fill ten minutes," which is far easier.

**Park on the edge.**

When you finish a session, leave a note about what comes next: "Next: the part about the bowl, with the detail about the kitchen." Starting again is easy when you know the next sentence.

**The first hour is a habit, not a battle.**

If you spend it on one sentence, then a section, then a timer, you are writing. The writer who does ten minutes a day beats the writer who waits for a perfect two-hour block.

Try it tomorrow: one sentence, the section you know, a ten-minute timer. That is the whole plan.

## Why this works

**The headline promises a specific outcome.** "Stop wasting your first hour" names a problem readers recognise. It is concrete, not vague.

**The subtitle makes the promise sharper** and sets up a curiosity gap: "the problem is not your willpower, it's your first move."

**The introduction is short and immediate.** Within a few lines, we are in the problem, not in a history of writing.

**Headings carry the structure.** A skimmer reading only the headings gets the whole method: start small, write the section you know, use a timer, park on the edge.

**Bullets and short paragraphs** make it easy to scan. No wall of text.

**It is specific and original**, not generic "tips." It has a named plan and a clear number (ten minutes).

**It ends with a next step** — a link to a related guide. That is the call to action.

## The structure to copy

1. **Headline** — a specific outcome.
2. **Subtitle** — sharpen the promise.
3. **Introduction** — put the reader in the problem.
4. **Numbered or sectioned steps** with clear headings.
5. **A concrete, memorable plan.**
6. **A next step or call to action.**

## Common mistakes

A vague headline ("Writing Tips"). A long introduction about the history of writing. Walls of text. Generic advice anyone could write. No clear next step.
