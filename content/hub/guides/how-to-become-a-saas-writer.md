SaaS writing is the niche most often recommended to writers who want to earn more. It is a real market with real money in it. It is also the one where the recommendation and the survey data disagree most sharply, and the disagreement is worth understanding before you commit a year to it.

## The uncomfortable number first

From UK market analysis built on the **ProCopywriters survey of 474 freelance copywriters**:

| Specialism | Day rate |
|---|---|
| **Financial services and fintech** | **£600–£850** |
| Technology and software | **£450–£750** |
| Overall UK average, all copywriting | £480 |

**Technology and software pays less than financial services**, despite tech having by far the louder reputation for paying well.

There is a structural reason, and it is not that tech undervalues writing:

> **A great many SaaS companies are startups, and startups have small budgets.**

A pre-Series-A company may genuinely want good writing and genuinely be unable to pay £700 a day for it. An insurer with a compliance department has both the need and the money.

## So "SaaS writing" is really two markets

This is the distinction that decides your income, and almost nobody draws it.

**Enterprise and scale-up B2B.** Real content budgets, established marketing teams, rates at the top of the range. The work skews technical: integration guides, security documentation, migration content, case studies.

**Early-stage startups.** Enthusiasm, equity offers, "exposure to our audience", and rates at or below general content. Often a poor use of a specialist's time however interesting the product.

Choosing between these two is a bigger determinant of your earnings than any amount of skill development. Most writers who report SaaS as disappointing were working the second market.

## Where the money actually is, ranked

From 2026 market analyses of freelance technical and SaaS work:

1. **Developer-facing content** — API documentation, integration guides, technical tutorials. **$80–$175/hour.** The smallest supply of competent writers anywhere in this field.
2. **Security and compliance content** — SOC 2, ISO, data protection explainers. **$85–$160/hour**, growing fast.
3. **Technical white papers and research reports** — priced as marketing assets rather than documentation, often the best per-hour work available.
4. **Case studies** — consistently well paid because they require interviewing customers, and most writers avoid the coordination.
5. **Product and lifecycle email** — undervalued relative to its revenue impact.
6. **SEO blog content** — the most abundant and worst paid. This is the part of the market AI has compressed hardest.

The ordering tracks technical depth almost exactly. The further you sit from *"write a 1,500-word blog post about productivity"*, the better the rate.

## How to actually break in

**Pick a category, not "SaaS".** Developer tools, fintech infrastructure, healthtech, cybersecurity, martech — each has its own vocabulary and buyer. *"I write for SaaS"* says nothing and competes with everyone.

**Use the product before you write about it.** Almost every SaaS company has a free tier. Writing from a press release rather than from having logged in is instantly visible to a technical reader, and technical readers are the audience.

**Publish one deeply technical piece.** One genuinely expert article beats a portfolio of generic marketing content, because the client's real question is whether you understand the subject. Pick a product with weak documentation, write the page they should have written, and publish it.

**Learn the funnel vocabulary.** Knowing the difference between top-of-funnel education and bottom-of-funnel comparison content lets you talk about business impact instead of word count. That is how you escape per-word pricing.

**Read the security and compliance material.** SOC 2 and ISO 27001 explainers are dry, in constant demand, and most writers will not touch them. That is exactly why they pay.

## How to price it

**Do not quote per word.** SaaS clients think in projects and retainers, and per-word pricing punishes you for being concise — which is the whole skill in product writing. See [per word, day rate, or project fee](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/per-word-day-rate-or-project-fee/).

**Three approaches that work with a constrained startup budget:**

- **Scope down, do not rate down.** Fewer deliverables at your rate, not more at a discount. A discounted rate is very hard to reverse with the same client.
- **Price the outcome where it is measurable.** A case study that closes deals, or documentation that reduces support tickets, has a business case. Blog volume rarely does.
- **Retainers at a stated discount.** A defined monthly scope at 10–15% below your project rate is a fair trade for guaranteed income. An open-ended "a few pieces a month" is not.

## An honest comparison before you commit

If your goal is the highest rate per day, the survey data points at **financial services and insurance** (£600–£850) rather than SaaS — and that market is less crowded because writers find it boring. See [finance and insurance copywriting](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/finance-and-insurance-copywriting/).

If your goal is interesting work with technically literate clients and a clear path into higher-paying developer content, SaaS is a good choice — provided you aim at the enterprise end and not the seed-stage end.

Both are defensible. Choosing on reputation rather than data is not.
