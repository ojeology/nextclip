The single most common failure in a literature review is a structure that goes: Smith found this. Jones found that. Okonkwo found the other. That is an annotated bibliography with the annotations run together, and it is marked down every time.

A literature review is an argument about a field. Its job is to show what is known, where the field disagrees, and — crucially — what has not been settled, because that gap is the justification for your own work. It is organised by ideas, and sources appear inside the ideas.

## Reviewing versus listing

The test is what your paragraph is about. If the paragraph is about a study, you are listing. If the paragraph is about a question, a method, a disagreement or a theme, and studies appear as evidence, you are reviewing.

Listing: *Adeyemi (2019) surveyed 200 teachers and found X. Following this, Kaur (2021) surveyed 350 and found Y.*

Reviewing: *Survey evidence consistently reports X in urban schools (Adeyemi 2019; Kaur 2021; Mensah 2023), but every one of these studies drew its sample from state capitals, and the two rural studies that exist find the opposite (Bello 2020; Tran 2022). The disagreement may therefore be about sampling rather than about teaching.*

The second paragraph has a claim. That is the whole difference.

## The process

**Define the boundary first.** Which years, which languages, which disciplines, which kinds of study. Write it down. Without a boundary a literature review has no end, and this is the main reason people stall.

**Search systematically and record as you go.** Databases, keywords, dates. Keep a note of what you searched and when — for a formal review you may have to report it, and for any review you will otherwise repeat yourself.

**Read for the conversation, not the content.** As you read, note who agrees with whom, who is responding to whom, and where the field changed direction. This is what you are actually reviewing.

**Build a matrix.** A simple table: source, question, method, sample, finding, limitation. Fill it in as you read. When the themes emerge, they will emerge from the columns — usually from the method or limitation column.

**Group into themes.** Three to six. These become your sections. If a theme has one source in it, it is not a theme.

**Write from the matrix, not from the papers.** Have the matrix open and the papers closed. This is the single most effective trick for avoiding paragraph-per-source drift.

## Structuring it

**Thematically** is the default and usually the best: each section is a question or a debate.

**Chronologically** only if the field genuinely changed over time and that change is your point.

**Methodologically** when the disagreement is about how the work is done — common in health and psychology.

**Theoretically** when competing frameworks are the story.

Within each theme: state the claim, present the agreement, present the dissent, evaluate the quality of the evidence, and say what remains open.

## Ending on the gap

The final section must state what is missing, and your research question must be visibly the answer to it. A review that ends with a summary rather than a gap has not done its structural job — it may be an excellent essay and it is not yet a literature review.

## Common mistakes

One paragraph per source. Describing studies without evaluating them. Citing only work that agrees with you. Including a source because you read it, not because it is needed. Uncritical use of a source's own abstract. A gap that is merely "nobody has studied X in my country" without saying why that matters. Losing track of which idea belongs to which source — a genuine plagiarism risk, not just an untidiness.

## Practical tips

- Keep the matrix in a spreadsheet from day one; retrofitting it is miserable.
- Use a reference manager and let it do the formatting.
- Read the introductions of recent reviews in your field to see how the conversation is currently framed.
- If you cannot write the sentence "the field disagrees about ___", you have not read enough yet.
- Check whether your discipline expects a systematic review with a reported protocol — the standards are much stricter, and finding out late is expensive.
