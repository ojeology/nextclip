Poetry compresses feeling into the fewest, sharpest words. There is no single right way, but the same move drives most good poems: start with a real image or feeling, and use language precisely to make the reader feel it.

## What it is

A poem is a concentrated piece of writing that uses image, sound, rhythm and word choice to evoke feeling or meaning. It can rhyme or not, follow a form or not — the point is precision and effect.

## Why it matters

Poetry teaches compression and attention to language, which improves all your writing. It is also the most personal form: a way to say something in words that would not work any other way.

## How to write it

**Start with an image or a feeling, not a message.** Begin with something you can see, hear, or feel: a moment, an object, a memory. Poems grow from specific images, not from abstract ideas about "love" or "time."

**Show, do not explain.** Let the concrete image carry the meaning. Instead of "I felt lonely," show the lone light on, the empty chair, the unread message.

**Use precise word choice.** One exact noun or verb beats three vague ones. Notice sound — the way words fit together — as well as meaning.

**Let the line and rhythm do work.** Line breaks create emphasis and pacing. Where you break a line changes what the reader holds. Read aloud and trust your ear.

**Use the tools of poetry deliberately.** Imagery (sensory detail), metaphor and simile (comparing), sound (rhyme, alliteration, repetition). Use them when they serve the poem, not to decorate it.

**Do not force a rhyme.** A forced rhyme sounds worse than no rhyme. Free verse — prose-like lines that use rhythm and image — is fine, and common.

**Write it, then revise the best parts.** Cut the weakest lines. A poem is often strongest when it is shorter. Find the one line that is truly yours and build the poem around it.

## Common mistakes

Writing an abstract message with images padded on top. Telling the reader what to feel. Forcing a rhyme or a "poetic" tone. Explaining the poem after writing it. Keeping a weak first or last line.

## Practical tips

- Write a first draft freely, then cut ruthlessly.
- Read it aloud to hear its rhythm and the best line.
- End on your strongest line — save power for the close.
- Let it sit, then return with fresh eyes.
