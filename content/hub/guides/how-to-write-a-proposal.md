A proposal asks someone to say yes — to fund a project, approve a plan, or hire you. It succeeds when it is clear about the problem, the solution and the value, and when it makes the decision easy.

## What it is

A proposal is a document that presents a plan for approval. It is persuasive but also structured and factual, and it must give the reader everything needed to decide.

## How to write it

**State the problem up front.** The reader needs to understand what you are solving before they consider your solution. Open by naming the problem, its cost, and why it matters.

**Propose a clear solution.** Say specifically what you plan to do. Avoid vague statements; give the concrete steps, the scope and the expected outcome.

**Show the value.** Why is this worth it? Quantify the return where you can — savings, time, revenue, or impact. Tie the value to the reader's goal.

**Give a realistic plan and timeline.** A proposal with no plan or a fantasy timeline reads as unserious. Show what will happen, in what order, and by when.

**Be honest about cost and risk.** State the cost clearly and be realistic about limitations. Honesty builds the trust needed to get a yes.

**End with a clear ask.** Say exactly what you need — approval, a budget, a decision by a date — and make it easy to say yes.

## The structure to copy

1. **Summary** — the problem and your recommendation in a few lines.
2. **The problem** — what it is and why it matters.
3. **The solution** — what you propose, concretely.
4. **The value** — why it is worth it.
5. **The plan and timeline.**
6. **Cost and risks.**
7. **The ask** — what you need and by when.

## Common mistakes

Starting with your solution before the problem is clear. Being vague. Over-promising and under-costing. No plan or timeline. Asking for approval without a clear ask.

## Practical tips

- Write the summary after you know the details.
- Keep the reader's goal in focus throughout.
- Be realistic — an honest proposal is far more convincing than an inflated one.
