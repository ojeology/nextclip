The same facts can be written in a dozen different ways. Which one you choose depends on who is reading and how you want to come across.

## What it is

**Audience** is who will read it. **Tone** is the attitude your words convey (formal, warm, urgent, playful). **Voice** is your consistent, recognisable way of writing — the personality behind the words.

## Why it matters

Writing that ignores its audience is writing at no one. Matching your tone to the situation makes your message land; a mismatch (a joke in a formal report, stiff language in a friendly email) confuses the reader.

## Step-by-step guide

**Name your reader.** What do they know, what do they care about, and what do they need to do after reading? Write for a specific person, not "people in general."

**Decide what you want the reader to feel or do.** Inform them, persuade them, reassure them, or get them to act. Let that shape your tone.

**Choose a register.** Formal, neutral, or conversational. Professional reports and job applications are formal. A blog, a personal essay, or an email to a colleague is often conversational.

**Match your voice, not someone else's.** Write the way you genuinely write, within the register the situation needs. Your voice comes from your word choices, rhythm, and habits.

**Adjust length and vocabulary to the reader.** A technical reader accepts jargon; a general reader does not. A busy reader wants brevity.

**Check the tone after you write.** Ask: would my reader react the way I want? Does this sound like me, or like a generic article?

## Common mistakes

**Confusing tone with voice.** Tone changes per piece; voice stays yours. A friendly email and a formal report can share the same voice.

**Writing for an audience you have not defined.** "Readers" is too vague. Picture one real person.

**Matching the wrong register.** Using slang in a business document, or stiff corporate language in a personal note.

**Being so neutral you sound like no one.** A piece with no voice is forgettable.

## Practical tips

- Keep a note of the two or three publications whose voice you admire, and study not words but rhythm.
- Write the opening line last if the tone is tricky to settle.
- Swap words you would never say aloud for words you would.
- Read your work aloud to hear whether the tone is right.
