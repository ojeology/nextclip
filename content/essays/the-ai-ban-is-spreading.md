In September 2026 I read the submission guidelines of 33 paying literary and journalistic publications across the UK, Canada and Australia — every word, on the publication's own page, not on an aggregator. I was collecting rates. What I found instead was the fastest-moving policy shift in the market.

**Seven of the 33 now prohibit AI-written submissions outright.** That is 21%. Across BRYME's full database of 138 verified publications, 41 prohibit AI and another four require disclosure.

More telling than the count is the language.

## The wording has changed

Two years ago the standard formulation was cautious and procedural: *we ask that you disclose any use of AI tools.* It read like a data-protection notice.

The 2026 wording is not procedural. It is angry.

Island, the Tasmanian literary magazine, puts it like this on its live submissions page:

> Please don't send us anything you've written, rewritten or otherwise created using AI. **AI is destroying writers' income** and futures.

That is not a compliance clause. That is a magazine picking a side in an argument about its readers' livelihoods.

Prairie Fire, in Winnipeg, is broader still — it refuses "work that utilizes AI, algorithmic, machine-learning, or computer-generated text **of any sort**." Note what that sweeps in. Not just generated prose. Any machine-generated text.

## The strictest policy I found

The Fiction Desk, in the UK, has gone furthest, and it is worth quoting the mechanism rather than the sentiment. Their AI prohibition appears in **two** places: the submission form, where you confirm you did not use AI, and again in the publishing contract you sign if accepted.

And their definition explicitly includes **grammar and punctuation correction tools**.

Read that again if you use Grammarly. Under that policy, running your story through a grammar checker before submitting is a breach of the confirmation you signed.

I am not endorsing that line — I think it is drawn in a strange place, because a spellchecker and a language model are not the same object. But it is the actual policy of a paying market, it is contractual, and a writer who assumes "AI ban" means "don't have ChatGPT write it" is going to sign something they are already violating.

## Who else has drawn a line

| Publication | Country | Policy |
|---|---|---|
| Extra Teeth | UK | Prohibited |
| The Fiction Desk | UK | Prohibited, contractual, includes grammar tools |
| EVENT | Canada | Prohibited — names ChatGPT specifically |
| Prairie Fire | Canada | Prohibited — "of any sort" |
| Augur Magazine | Canada | Prohibited — breach of contract if discovered after acceptance |
| Island | Australia | Prohibited |
| Aurealis | Australia | "We have a no AI policy" |

Augur's construction is the one with teeth. AI use is not merely grounds for rejection at the slush stage — it is **a breach of contract if the piece has already been accepted**. That means the exposure does not end when you get the acceptance email.

The Malahat Review takes a quieter route, noting its editorial boards "are not interested in AI-generated work as such." The /tEmz/ Review takes the most thoughtful position of any I read: AI use is permitted where there is a legitimate artistic reason, provided you state the extent and the reasons in your cover letter, and it will be disclosed to readers. Undisclosed use is treated as a serious matter.

That is the only policy in the set that distinguishes between *using a tool* and *passing off a machine's work as your own*. I suspect it is where most of the market ends up.

## Why this is happening now, and not in 2023

The obvious reading is aesthetic — editors are tired of slush piles full of competent, weightless prose. That is real. Several editors have said so publicly.

But the Island quote points at the actual driver, and it is economic. These are magazines that pay £20 to £675 a piece out of grant funding and subscriptions. Their entire reason to exist is that human writing is worth paying for. A magazine that accepted generated submissions would be arguing against its own budget.

The AI ban is not a technology policy. It is a market defending its price floor.

## What this means if you are submitting

**Read the guidelines every single time.** This is the practical point. The policy landscape changed inside eighteen months and it is still moving. A market that had no AI clause when you last submitted may have one now.

**"AI ban" is not one thing.** It ranges from "don't have a model write it" to "don't run a grammar checker over it." The distance between those is enormous and the word "AI" does not tell you which you are agreeing to.

**Check whether it is contractual.** A guideline is a preference. A contract clause is a legal undertaking. The Fiction Desk and Augur have both moved it into the contract.

**Disclosure is not always available as an option.** Writers often assume that being upfront resolves it. In most of the policies above, it does not — there is no disclosure route, only a prohibition.

**Assume enforcement will improve.** Detection is unreliable today and editors know it. What they have done instead is shift the risk onto you contractually, which does not require detection to work. It requires only that you signed.

## The part nobody says out loud

There is an obvious tension in a market that bans AI while paying £20 a poem. Writers are being asked to hold a line that is expensive for them to hold, by publications that cannot pay them much for holding it.

I do not think that makes the editors wrong. Island's argument — that this is about writers' income — is coherent, and the magazines drawing the hardest lines are frequently the ones paying the most per piece relative to their size.

But it does mean the burden is landing unevenly. It lands on the writer, in the form of a contractual undertaking, at rates that have not moved much in a decade.

Worth knowing before you tick the box.
