12 Movies Like Deadpool & Wolverine, Picked for Different Moods

Updated 9 October 2026 · Spoiler-light recommendations · BRYME Entertainment

Choose Deadpool or Deadpool 2 for Wade’s humour, The Suicide Squad for violent ensemble chaos, or Logan for a serious Wolverine story. Free Guy is the better direction if you enjoyed the self-aware jokes more than the bloodshed. No single follow-up matches every part of Deadpool & Wolverine.

This guide separates four reasons you might want another film: irreverent comedy, a mismatched partnership, Wolverine’s history, or superhero crossovers. Each recommendation explains both the overlap and the difference. Similarity is BRYME’s judgement, not a studio claim or a numerical score.

1. Deadpool: the most direct place to start

Choose it for: Wade’s fourth-wall-breaking humour and an action film willing to mock its own genre. If Deadpool & Wolverine was your first encounter with the character, the original gives the jokes a more personal context instead of asking you to learn the surrounding franchise first.

What changes: it is a more concentrated introduction to Wade, not the same crossover experience. That smaller focus is a benefit if the constant references in the later film were distracting. Official film page.

2. Deadpool 2: more Wade, with a broader group around him

Choose it for: the clearest continuation of the first film’s mix of jokes, action and reluctant emotional investment. This is the straightforward answer when “more like Deadpool & Wolverine” really means “more Deadpool,” rather than a different hero with a similar tone.

What changes: the ensemble and conflicts differ from Wade’s partnership with Wolverine. Watch the first Deadpool before this one if you want a simple character-led order. You do not need to turn it into a marathon of every Marvel release. Official film page.

3. The Suicide Squad: for unruly ensemble action

Choose it for: a group of dangerous, damaged characters whose bad behaviour does not prevent the story from asking you to care about them. The appeal is the friction between absurdity, brutal action and unexpected feeling.

What changes: this is the 2021 film The Suicide Squad, not 2016’s similarly named Suicide Squad. Its ensemble matters more than a single wisecracking narrator. Choose it for chaotic team dynamics rather than expecting Wade’s particular relationship with the audience. Official film page.

4. Logan: for Wolverine, not for another comedy

Choose it for: a weary Wolverine and a more intimate story. The official synopsis places Logan in hiding, caring for an ailing Professor X, before a young mutant disrupts their fragile refuge.

What changes: its violence carries a sombre weight rather than functioning mainly as a punchline. If you want more jokes, choose something else on this list. If Wolverine was the reason you connected with the newer film, Logan offers the strongest change of perspective here. Official synopsis.

5. Free Guy: for self-aware comedy with less brutality

Choose it for: a character discovering that his ordinary life belongs to an artificial world. The film follows a bank teller who learns he is a background player in a video game and tries to become the hero of his own story.

What changes: the mood is more upbeat and less abrasive than Deadpool’s. It is a better choice for the pleasure of noticing the rules of a fictional world than for savage fights. “Less brutal” is a comparison, not an assurance of suitability for every child; check your local content guidance. Official synopsis.

6. Kingsman: The Secret Service: for outrageous action

Choose it for: a spy story that turns polished manners and elaborate action into a deliberately excessive combination. A young recruit enters a secret organisation’s competitive training programme while a wider threat develops.

What changes: its framework is espionage rather than superhero continuity. The comparison is about stylised action and irreverence, not matching powers or characters. This is a useful branch away from Marvel if you want the energy without another multiverse explanation. Official synopsis.

7. Thor: Ragnarok: for bickering heroes and comic spectacle

Choose it for: a colourful superhero adventure that makes room for awkwardness, jokes and characters undercutting one another. The recommendation is strongest if you enjoyed the comic friction between powerful people more than Deadpool’s explicit humour.

What changes: its tone is less profane and it does not depend on Wade-style direct address to the audience. It remains its own fantasy adventure. Do not expect a substitute Deadpool; expect a different comic use of a familiar hero. Official film page.

8. Guardians of the Galaxy Vol. 2: for jokes with family conflict underneath

Choose it for: a quarrelling group whose humour sits alongside emotional needs they would rather not admit. The official story follows the Guardians as they try to keep their new family together and confront the mystery of Peter Quill’s parentage.

What changes: music, cosmic adventure and the ensemble carry more of the film than a single double act. Start with the first Guardians film if you want the relationships to arrive with their full context. Choose this for sentiment beneath the jokes, not for comparable gore. Official synopsis.

9. Ant-Man: for a lighter comic adventure

Choose it for: a superhero story with a heist structure and humour built around scale. It is the quieter recommendation on this list: the fun comes from a smaller operation and ordinary-person uncertainty rather than the biggest possible crossover.

What changes: it is not a close match for Deadpool’s explicit jokes or violent excess. Pick it deliberately as a lighter change of pace. If those were the only elements you wanted to repeat, the first two Deadpool films are better choices. Official film page.

10. Ant-Man and the Wasp: for a different two-hero partnership

Choose it for: shared problem-solving, comic complications and action that plays with changes in size. It belongs here for the partnership and brisker comic energy, not because its characters behave like Wade and Logan.

What changes: the relationship is not built around the same antagonistic, profane double act. Watching Ant-Man first is the simpler introduction. Treat this as a companion recommendation to the preceding entry rather than another claim that all superhero sequels feel alike. Official film page.

11. Spider-Man: No Way Home: for the superhero-crossover feeling

Choose it for: the excitement of a superhero story whose consequences reach beyond its immediate world. Its starting problem is personal: Peter’s revealed identity places his responsibilities in conflict with his ordinary life and endangers people he cares about.

What changes: its emotional centre is more earnest than Deadpool’s. Avoid cast-reveal clips if you want to keep its surprises intact. This is a match for the event-film sensation, not for the same comic voice or level of violence. Official synopsis.

12. X-Men: Days of Future Past: for the wider mutant ensemble

Choose it for: the X-Men side of your interest in Wolverine. The official synopsis describes an ensemble struggling for survival across two time periods, giving this recommendation a different basis from the comic picks above.

What changes: it is a serious ensemble adventure, not a foul-mouthed buddy comedy. Choose it when the characters’ shared history interests you more than another run of meta jokes. Prior familiarity with the X-Men adds context, but this guide does not promise that one film explains every continuity question. Official synopsis.

How to choose without watching all twelve

  • More of the same lead character: Deadpool, then Deadpool 2.
  • Violent comic ensemble: The Suicide Squad.
  • Wolverine’s emotional side: Logan.
  • Self-aware but more upbeat: Free Guy.
  • Spy-action excess: Kingsman: The Secret Service.
  • Comic hero partnerships: Thor: Ragnarok or Ant-Man and the Wasp.
  • Team feeling and emotion: Guardians of the Galaxy Vol. 2.
  • Crossover or mutant ensemble: Spider-Man: No Way Home or X-Men: Days of Future Past.
