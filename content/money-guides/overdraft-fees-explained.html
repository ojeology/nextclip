Overdraft fees were, for years, among the most complained-about charges in banking. The landscape has genuinely improved — many big US banks removed non-sufficient-funds fees entirely between 2022 and 2024, and UK rules banned fixed overdraft charges in favour of a single interest rate — but overdrafts still cost money, and knowing the fee types that remain is worth ten minutes.

What an overdraft is

An overdraft is short-term borrowing attached to a current or checking account: when a payment takes the balance below zero, the bank covers it and the account is overdrawn. An arranged overdraft is agreed in advance with a limit and a price; an unarranged one happens anyway and has historically been the expensive case. The FDIC's consumer resources cover how deposit accounts and overdraft programmes work from the regulator's side.

The fee types that remain

Four shapes to recognise on a statement. A per-item overdraft fee, charged each time the bank pays a transaction that overdraws you — three purchases on a bad day can mean three fees. A returned-item (NSF) fee, charged when the bank refuses the transaction instead; this is the fee many US banks eliminated. An extended-overdraft fee, charged for staying overdrawn beyond a set number of days. And an overdraft-transfer fee, the price of an optional link that pulls money from savings or a credit line to cover the gap — usually the cheapest option when it exists. The CFPB's consumer tools explain how to compare these across banks, because they vary more than interest rates do.

The opt-in rule, and processing order

In the US, banks may not charge overdraft fees on one-off debit-card and ATM transactions unless you have opted in; cheque and automatic-bill overdrafts sit outside that rule. How transactions are posted — largest-first, for instance — used to maximise fees and now draws regulatory scrutiny, but order still decides how many fees a bad day produces. In the UK, FCA rules since 2020 require overdraft pricing to be a single annual interest rate with no fixed fees, so the British question is the rate and the limit, not a fee schedule.

How to never meet these fees

Three defences, in order of strength. A low-balance alert set well above zero — not at zero, because by then the fee has often already triggered. A buffer in the budget: keeping a deliberate floor in the account, which is what 50/30/20 budgeting structurally provides. And the real answer, a small cash cushion that absorbs the bad week entirely — the first £/$500 of an emergency fund exists precisely for this. Repeated overdraft use is also reported through your account history and can shape lending decisions; how credit scores work covers what lenders actually see.
