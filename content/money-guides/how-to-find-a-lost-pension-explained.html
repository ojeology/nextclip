Retirement money goes missing in perfectly ordinary ways: you change employer, the provider changes name, the statement goes to an address you left years ago, and a pot worth several thousand pounds or dollars stops being anyone's daily problem. The search to find it is free in most places and takes an afternoon. The part people get wrong is who to search with.

Why pots go missing

Four mechanisms account for almost every lost pension. Job churn is the biggest: each employer switch can leave behind a workplace pot that nobody manages afterwards. Provider change is next — pensions are regularly transferred between administrators, and scheme names on old paperwork stop matching anything you can search for. Address drift does the quiet damage, because annual statements are the only reminder many people ever get, and a statement that bounces usually means a pot stops being serviced. Small pots round it off: a pot with a low balance feels not worth chasing, which is exactly how they accumulate into meaningful money nobody owns.

The search order that works

Start with your own records, not a search engine. Old payslips and employment contracts give you the employer name and the years you worked there, which is the key every tracing service needs. Then work through the free official routes for your country: in the UK the government runs a free pension tracing service that supplies current contact details for schemes based on employer or scheme name; in the US the free routes are the plan administrator named on any old statement, the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation for terminated defined-benefit plans, and your state's unclaimed property database, where forgotten retirement balances end up. Only then contact providers directly with employer name, your full name including any previous names, date of birth and national insurance or Social Security number.

What paid tracing services actually do

A whole industry exists to do the free search for you. Asset locators and "pension tracing" companies will usually find what the official service finds, and charge for it — sometimes as a percentage of the pot, which on a modest balance can be a large sum for an afternoon of phone calls. The distinction worth holding onto: paying for convenience is a choice, paying for access is a scam. No legitimate provider, tracing service or regulator will ever ask you to transfer a pension into an account they name, and none of them need your online banking login.

What to do once you find several

Multiple small pots is a maintenance problem as much as a value problem: several sets of charges, several sets of statements, several chances for one to go missing again. Consolidating can reduce admin and cost, and there are real counterweights — some older schemes carry guarantees, protected retirement ages or employer contributions that would be given up on transfer, and exiting a scheme can trigger charges. The honest sequence is to get the facts on each pot in writing (current value, charges, guarantees, any protected features), compare them side by side, and take regulated advice before moving anything with a guarantee attached. Getting the facts is free; only the decision needs to be careful.

Stopping the next one going missing

Every provider holds a contact-details record, and updating it takes a phone call. Add that to every house move and job change, keep one folder with every annual statement you have ever received, and check once a year that each pot has a current address on it. That single habit is the difference between a retirement pot you can find in five minutes and one that takes a decade to trace.
