The deposit is the gatekeeping number of home ownership: bigger than most people expect, needed years before it is spent, and — because it sets the terms of everything that follows — worth more than its face value. The good news is that it is also the most mechanically solvable problem in personal finance. It is a target, a timeline and a place to park the money. That is the whole guide.

Why the number matters more than it looks

The deposit does two jobs at once. It buys equity — your share of the property on day one. And it sets the loan-to-value ratio, which the lender uses to price your risk: the bigger the deposit, the lower the LTV, the cheaper the mortgage rate, the smaller the monthly payment, and (in the US below roughly 20% down) the absence of mortgage insurance — a monthly premium protecting the lender, not you. A larger deposit therefore saves money twice: less borrowed, and borrowed more cheaply. The counterweight is opportunity cost — cash sunk into a deposit earns nothing, while the compounding it could have done elsewhere is real. The honest target is the smallest deposit that reaches a good rate tier and avoids insurance, not the largest you can scrape.

Timeline: the maths, worked

Start from the number, not the monthly: pick a realistic property price, decide the LTV tier you want, and the deposit falls out. Then run it through the savings goal calculator — but at a savings rate, not an investment return: deposit money has a deadline measured in years, and years is too short for equity risk. The sinking funds guide has the structure: a dedicated pot, a monthly number, a date. Then automate it on payday, before the money sees the inside of your spending. If the timeline stretches past five years, the later tranches can work harder — but the money you need in year two should not be invested at all.

Where the money waits

Deposit savings want safety and the best guaranteed rate: a high-yield savings account or fixed-term deposits, not the market — the ladder guide shows where it sits in the order of operations (emergency fund first, always: a deposit fund you raid in a crisis is not a deposit fund). Some jurisdictions add wrappers worth filling: the UK Lifetime ISA adds a government bonus to first-home savings within its annual and lifetime caps — free money with house-shaped strings attached, worth the paperwork. The US has no direct equivalent; the CFPB's home-buying guides make the same point structurally — shop the mortgage, not just the house.

Gifts, and their rules

Family help is increasingly part of deposit maths, and it comes with paperwork: lenders want gift letters confirming the money is not a loan (it affects affordability), both tax systems watch large gifts for inheritance reasons — the inheritance tax guide covers the seven-year and exemption rules — and the giver should never be left asset-poor by the gesture. What not to do is equally short: do not raid retirement wrappers (the penalties and lost decades are covered in the IRA guide), and do not put the deposit on credit. The deposit is the one savings goal where boring is the entire strategy — a target, a date, a high-yield pot, and patience doing the rest.
