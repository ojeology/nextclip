10 Shows Like Alice in Borderland You Should Watch Next

Updated 9 October 2026 · Spoiler-light recommendations · BRYME Entertainment

Start with Squid Game for deadly competitions, The 8 Show for manipulation inside a controlled game, or The Devil’s Plan for real contestants solving strategic challenges. If survival horror mattered more to you than puzzles, try All of Us Are Dead or Sweet Home.

A good follow-up does not need to copy Alice in Borderland. It needs to reproduce the part you enjoyed: learning dangerous rules, deciding whom to trust or watching an ordinary group improvise under pressure. This list separates those qualities instead of treating every violent thriller as the same experience.

The comparisons and watch order are BRYME’s editorial judgement. Linked sources establish the shows and their premises, not an objective similarity score. All ten entries are series; anime and reality competition are clearly labelled.

  1. Squid Game — deadly competitions
  2. No Game No Life — rule-based strategy
  3. The 8 Show — manipulation and unequal rules
  4. The Devil’s Plan — real strategy games
  5. Tomodachi Game — trust under pressure
  6. 3% — dystopian selection trials
  7. All of Us Are Dead — group survival
  8. The 100 — survival on a larger scale
  9. Sweet Home — monster horror
  10. Kingdom — horror and political stakes

1. Squid Game: the closest starting point for deadly games

Format: live-action drama. Financially desperate contestants enter a competition built around children’s games with lethal consequences. The straightforward rules make the choices between self-preservation and loyalty easy to grasp, even when those choices become unbearable. Official Netflix synopsis.

Why it fits: understanding the challenge is only half the problem; choosing whom to trust is the other half. The difference: its social and financial pressures are more central than Alice in Borderland’s abandoned-city mystery. Choose it for the human stakes, not because you expect the same world or mythology.

2. No Game No Life: for viewers who enjoy solving the rules

Format: fantasy anime. Siblings with exceptional gaming abilities enter a world where games decide conflicts. The appeal is watching them examine the rules and exploit what other players overlook. HIDIVE’s overview describes that game-governed premise. [3]

Why it fits: it rewards attention to loopholes and strategy. The difference: the flamboyant fantasy setting and comic tone are a substantial shift from live-action survival suspense. It is a better choice for the puzzle-solving part of Alice in Borderland than for its atmosphere of vulnerability.

3. The 8 Show: for manipulation inside a designed system

Format: live-action drama. Eight people enter a mysterious multistorey building and a show in which time earns money. They must work out how the arrangement operates while living alongside the other participants. [1]

Why it fits: the tension comes from discovering that cooperation and self-interest do not always point in the same direction. The difference: expect a confined social experiment and dark satire rather than a sequence of elaborate action arenas. This is the pick when you want to argue about the participants’ decisions after an episode.

4. The Devil’s Plan: strategy without fictional death games

Format: reality competition, not scripted survival drama. Real contestants compete in games of wit and strategy. Alliances, information and the way a player interprets a rule can change a round. Official Netflix synopsis.

Why it fits: viewers who pause Alice in Borderland to discuss what they would do may enjoy following real decisions. The difference: contestants are playing for a prize, not facing the fictional lethal stakes of Borderland. Do not start it expecting an action thriller. It is a different genre selected for one shared pleasure: thinking through the game.

5. Tomodachi Game: when friendship is the difficult part

Format: anime. Missing school-trip money and suspicion among friends set the story in motion. The anime’s premise is documented in its series listing. [1]

Why it fits: it shifts attention from “Can this person solve the challenge?” to “Can this person be trusted?” Choose it for deception, secrets and the pressure a game places on relationships. The difference: its appeal is psychological suspicion rather than the physical spectacle of escaping through a deserted city. The animation format also changes how that tension is presented.

6. 3%: selection trials with a dystopian purpose

Format: live-action science fiction. In a divided society, candidates compete for a chance to join the small minority allowed into a privileged world. Netflix’s synopsis describes the contrast between the impoverished population and the elite island. [1]

Why it fits: tests become a way of exposing values, weaknesses and competing ideas of fairness. The difference: the trials are part of a larger political system. This is a useful step away from a pure death-game story if you want to explore who designs the rules and who benefits from them.

7. All of Us Are Dead: for a group improvising under pressure

Format: live-action horror. A high school becomes the centre of a zombie outbreak, trapping students who must try to escape. Official Netflix synopsis.

Why it fits: frightened people have to cooperate before they have enough information, and a safe-looking room may become a trap. The difference: the immediate problem is an outbreak, not a game with announced rules. Pick this when Alice in Borderland’s teamwork and urgent escape scenes mattered more than its puzzles. Expect graphic horror rather than a gentler alternative.

8. The 100: survival that expands into leadership and conflict

Format: live-action science fiction. The series follows survivors on a post-apocalyptic Earth; Warner Bros describes a landscape of leaders, followers and warriors. Official series page.

Why it fits: survival becomes a question of whom a group follows and what it will sacrifice. The difference: this is a broader ensemble story, not a succession of self-contained games. Choose it if you want to spend longer with shifting alliances and rival groups; it is less suitable if your main request is another tightly focused puzzle competition.

9. Sweet Home: for the feeling that nowhere is safe

Format: live-action monster horror. People begin turning into monsters while survivors struggle to stay alive and retain their humanity. The opening episodes centre on residents of an apartment building. Official synopsis and episode guide.

Why it fits: a shared refuge forces very different people to make decisions together under threat. The difference: body horror and transformations replace the clean boundaries of a game. It is a match for fear, uncertainty and group survival—not for viewers looking only for solvable logical challenges.

10. Kingdom: for survival horror with wider consequences

Format: live-action historical horror. A crown prince confronts a mysterious plague while questions around an ill king deepen a political crisis. Official Netflix synopsis.

Why it fits: the danger keeps expanding beyond the immediate escape, and decisions made by powerful people affect those trying to survive. The difference: the historical setting and political struggle matter as much as the horror. There are no Borderland-style game rounds. Start here if you want a larger mystery around a frightening threat.

Which one should you watch first?

  • Most direct death-game match: Squid Game.
  • Social manipulation: The 8 Show or Tomodachi Game.
  • Rules and strategy: No Game No Life; The Devil’s Plan if you want real contestants.
  • Dystopian selection: 3%.
  • Horror and cooperation: All of Us Are Dead, Sweet Home or Kingdom.
  • A larger survival ensemble: The 100.
