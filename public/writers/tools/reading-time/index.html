Reading time calculator.

Estimate the reading time of your text at a typical pace (200 words per minute), so you can match length to the format.

About this tool

How to use the reading time calculator.

What it does. Estimates how long a piece takes to read, from its word count at a typical silent-reading speed.

How to use it. Paste the text to see the estimate. The usual assumption is roughly 200-250 words a minute; treat the figure as a band, not a promise - dense technical writing reads slower than a light list, and that is fine.

Why it matters. Reading time is a promise made next to the headline. Estimating it honestly, and then delivering it, is a small trust signal that keeps readers coming back.

Your data. The tool runs entirely in your browser - text you paste never leaves the page, and nothing is uploaded to a server.

Good to know

The estimate assumes silent reading - reading aloud runs at roughly a third of the speed, so a five-minute read is closer to fifteen presented. Podcasters and video hosts: budget accordingly.

Where this fits

Counting tools answer one question precisely: how much? They matter at the boundaries - the limit on a form, the length an editor asked for, the session you promised yourself. Used once, a counter answers a question; used as a habit, it keeps your work inside the frames that get it read.

The Reading Time Estimator And Its Assumptions

The reading time estimator reports how long a text takes to read — by common reading rates and by the pace the writer sets — the figure that shapes everything from web layout to talk slots.

Paste the text and the estimator reports the word count against the standard silent-reading rates — roughly two hundred to two hundred and fifty words a minute for adults reading non-fiction — producing a range rather than a single false minute. The tool also computes read-aloud time at speaking pace for scripts and talks.

The range is the honest output: reading speed varies by reader, difficulty and purpose, and any tool that prints one number to the second is laundering an average into a promise. The estimator shows the rate beside the time so the figure can be adjusted to the actual audience.

Reading time is an estimate and the tool’s rates are population averages, not measurements of the specific reader. Technical prose reads slower, familiar subjects faster, and skimming changes everything. The estimator prices the ticket; it cannot know who is riding.

The working use is editorial planning: the web layout that puts the estimate above the fold, the talk slot that must fit a script, the newsletter that promises a five-minute read. Writers who state reading time accurately build trust with readers — and the range keeps the promise honest.

How the tool treats your text

  • Runs in the browser: the text never leaves the page unless the tool says otherwise
  • Free for individual use; bulk and API use by written permission
  • Every result states what was counted and how, beside the result
  • Honest limits published on the page, not buried in a footer
  • Method reviewed and dated on the desk’s editorial calendar

Background context cross-checked against the encyclopaedic record where a reference helps. The desk’s standard applies here as everywhere: the arithmetic is shown, the method is stated and the limits are named. The tool is one instrument on a working writer’s bench: it handles the mechanical layer so the writer’s attention stays on the argument, the evidence and the voice. Everything the tool counts, it counts the same way every time, which is more than any tired eye at the end of a deadline can promise. When the tool and the writer disagree, the writer wins — but the disagreement is worth having, because it means the choice was made rather than missed. The tool runs locally in the page, needs no account and forgets the text when the tab closes, which is the privacy posture a working draft deserves. Reviewed 2026-09-28.
