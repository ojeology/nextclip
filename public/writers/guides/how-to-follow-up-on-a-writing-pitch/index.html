How to follow up on a writing pitch

When to wait, what to say in a follow-up, and how to treat silence without burning bridges.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

In this guide
  1. Wait a reasonable time
  2. Keep it short
  3. Confirm you can try elsewhere
  4. Treat silence as a sign
  5. Move on without burning bridges

Following up is normal, but it has to be done well.

Wait a reasonable time. If the publication states a response window, respect it. A short, polite follow-up after a couple of weeks is reasonable.

Keep it short. One or two lines: reference your pitch, ask if they had a chance to consider it, and offer to adjust.

Confirm you can pitch elsewhere. Many markets appreciate knowing you are holding the idea for them. Make it easy for them to say yes.

Treat silence as a sign. If there is no response after a follow-up or two, that is usually a no. BRYME does not invent response times — it records when they are not stated.

Move on without burning bridges. Publications have small teams and big inboxes. A later, better pitch to the same market is fine.

Desk notes: the follow-up, word for word

The guide above gives the timing; here is the wording, because follow-up anxiety is mostly drafting anxiety. A professional follow-up is three sentences: restate the pitch in one line ("I'm following up on my 12 May pitch on X"), ask the question plainly ("do you have a sense of whether it might fit?"), and offer the exit gracefully ("if it's not a fit, no reply needed — I'll take the idea elsewhere after [date]"). No apology, no sarcasm, no second pitch smuggled in.

Timing lives in data, not nerves: if the publication states a window, the desk records it on the opportunity page with the date we last read it; if it does not, The Submission Grinder's crowd-reported averages are the fairest proxy. The dedicated timing guide and the tracker turn this into arithmetic.

And the psychological rule that keeps pipelines healthy: silence is data, not insult. Magazines with volunteer slush readers can sit on submissions for a quarter without malice. Follow up once or twice as prescribed, then move the idea — the desk's rejection guide covers the rerouting.

The three follow-up sentences, filled in

Because the wording is where everyone stalls, here is the desk's fill-in-the-blanks version: subject — "Follow-up: [original subject, unchanged]". Line one — "Following up on my pitch of [date] about [one-line description]." Line two — the ask, plainly: "Is this something you'd like to see?" or "Do you have a sense of timing on your end?" Line three — the graceful exit: "If it's not a fit, no reply needed — I'll take it elsewhere after [date two weeks out]." Send weekday morning in their time zone. That is the whole email; every additional sentence costs more than it buys.

The date in line three is doing real work — it converts your follow-up into a scheduling fact the editor can act on with one glance, and it hands you the moral right to move the piece without a withdrawal drama. The machinery around this note — when it goes, what the market's window was — lives in the tracker and the timing guide.

The Three Sentences, Filled In

Following up on a pitch is a professional courtesy with timing rules: wait the stated window, send three sentences that add value, and treat silence as information. The templates here are filled in and ready to adapt.

  • When to wait The guidelines' window is the only clock that matters.
  • The three sentences A reminder, a value-add, and an easy exit, in order.
  • What silence means Two unanswered follow-ups is an answer.
  • Never in anger The follow-up is remembered longer than the pitch.
  • The value-add One new fact or one new idea keeps the thread alive.
  • Timing discipline A calendar beats a memory for follow-ups.

Follow-up is a genre with its own grammar: brief, useful and gracious. The three filled-in sentences do the work of a whole worried week. Where the facts on this page come from: the practical claims come from the desk's own testing and notes, the background facts are cross-checked against the encyclopaedic record where a reference helps, and anything the evidence contradicts has been cut rather than softened. The guidance here is general and dated: prices, rules and product behaviour change, so re-check anything time-sensitive against current sources before acting on it. Reviewed 2026-09-28.
