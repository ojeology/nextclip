Which format do I need?

Cover letter vs personal statement vs motivation letter

Three documents about yourself, sent to three different kinds of reader, doing three different jobs.

Beginner Start here — assumes no prior knowledge.

Every one of these is a piece of writing about you, attached to an application, arguing that you should be chosen. That is where the similarity ends. Reuse one as another and it reads as recycled, because it is: the cover letter is about a job, the personal statement is about you, and the motivation letter is about the fit between you and one particular institution.

Cover letter

Why you can do this specific job, for this specific employer.

Typical length: One page, three or four paragraphs

Who reads it: A recruiter or hiring manager reading it beside your CV

Voice: Professional, warm, specific. Business-letter conventions.

Shape: Which role and how you found it, your strongest relevant evidence, why this employer, a clear close.

Use it when: Job applications, almost always, even when described as optional.

Personal statement

Who you are, what formed your interest, and where you are going.

Typical length: Strictly capped — UCAS allows 4,000 characters; others 500–1,000 words

Who reads it: An admissions tutor reading several hundred in a sitting

Voice: Reflective and personal, but evidenced. Story in service of a claim.

Shape: Why this subject, what you have done about it, what you learned, what you bring.

Use it when: University applications, scholarships, some graduate schemes, professional training.

Motivation letter

Why this programme, at this institution, and why now.

Typical length: One to two pages

Who reads it: A selection committee comparing you against the programme's aims

Voice: Formal, structured, letter conventions. Often written in a second language.

Shape: Your background, why this field, why this specific programme and its staff, your plan afterwards.

Use it when: European master's and PhD applications, Erasmus, DAAD, Chevening, Fulbright and similar.

Decide quickly

Which one do you actually need?

  • There is a job advert → cover letter.
  • You are applying to study → personal statement or motivation letter.
  • The application is UK undergraduate through UCAS → personal statement, and one statement goes to every course you list.
  • The application is to a named European programme → motivation letter, rewritten for each one.
  • You must name specific supervisors or modules → motivation letter.
  • A character or word limit is enforced by the form → personal statement, and the limit is the brief.

Common confusions

The questions that come up every time.

Can I reuse one letter across applications?

Reuse the structure and your strongest evidence, never the paragraph explaining why them. That paragraph is the only part that cannot be recycled, it is the part committees read most closely, and a generic version is worse than none — it proves you did not look.

How personal should a personal statement be?

Personal enough to be unmistakably yours, and always in service of a claim about your suitability. A hardship is relevant when it explains a grade or shows what you can withstand. It is not relevant as an appeal for sympathy, and admissions tutors are practised at telling the two apart.

Should a cover letter repeat my CV?

No. The CV lists what you did; the letter explains what one or two of those things mean for this employer. If a sentence in your letter would sit comfortably on your CV, cut it and write the interpretation instead.
