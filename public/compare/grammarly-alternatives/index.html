Grammarly alternatives

What Grammarly actually does well, where it is weak, and the free and paid tools worth using instead or alongside it.

Beginner Start here — assumes no prior knowledge.

Grammarly is the default, and for a lot of people it is genuinely the right answer — it catches real errors, it works everywhere, and the free tier is useful. The reasons to look elsewhere are specific: it pushes a house style you may not want, the premium tier is expensive relative to what it adds for a confident writer, and it sends your text to a server, which is a problem for confidential or unpublished work. BRYME's own tools are listed below where they overlap, and they are free and run entirely in your browser — they are also narrower, and this page says where.

Grammarly

Broad grammar, spelling and style checking across every app you use.

Typical length: Free tier; premium is a monthly subscription

Who reads it: Anyone who wants one tool that works in the browser, in Word and in email

Voice: Prescriptive. Suggests a clean, corporate register by default.

Shape: Browser extension, desktop app, web editor, Word add-in.

Use it when: You want one always-on checker and are comfortable with your text being processed on a server.

LanguageTool

Open-source-rooted checker with strong support for languages other than English.

Typical length: Free tier; paid tier cheaper than Grammarly

Who reads it: Multilingual writers, and anyone who wants a self-hostable option

Voice: Less prescriptive than Grammarly. Fewer style opinions.

Shape: Extension, add-ins, API, and a self-hosted server option.

Use it when: You write in more than one language, or you need the data to stay on your own infrastructure.

Hemingway Editor

Readability rather than correctness — sentence length, passive voice, adverbs.

Typical length: Free in-browser; one-off payment for the desktop app

Who reads it: Writers whose problem is density, not grammar

Voice: Blunt. Colour-codes anything hard to read.

Shape: Paste-in editor with a grade-level score.

Use it when: Your grammar is fine and your sentences are too long.

BRYME tools

Individual free checks that run entirely in your browser.

Typical length: Free, no account, nothing uploaded

Who reads it: Writers who want one specific check without a subscription

Voice: None imposed — they report, they do not rewrite.

Shape: Separate tools: readability, passive voice, filler words, clichés, repetition, sentence length.

Use it when: You want a specific diagnostic, or your text cannot leave your machine.

Decide quickly

Which one do you actually need?

  • You want one tool that works everywhere and will pay for it → Grammarly.
  • You write in more than one language → LanguageTool.
  • Your sentences are long and dense → Hemingway, or BRYME's sentence-length checker.
  • The text is confidential, unpublished or under NDA → a tool that does not upload. BRYME's tools and self-hosted LanguageTool qualify; Grammarly does not.
  • You are on a student or Nigerian-naira budget → the free tiers plus BRYME cover most of it.
  • You already write well and want a diagnostic, not a rewriter → BRYME's individual checks.
  • You need it inside Microsoft Word → Grammarly or LanguageTool.

Common confusions

The questions that come up every time.

Does Grammarly make you a better writer?

It makes your text cleaner, which is not the same thing. It reliably catches typos, agreement errors and genuinely tangled sentences. It cannot tell you that your argument does not hold, that your opening is boring, or that the piece is 400 words too long — and those are usually what stands between a draft and a commission.

Is it safe to paste confidential work into a checker?

Read the terms for whichever you use, and assume text sent to a server may be retained. For unpublished manuscripts, client work under NDA, legal drafts or anything covered by a data-protection obligation, use a tool that processes locally. This is the single strongest argument for browser-only tools.

Should I accept every suggestion?

No, and accepting all of them is how prose becomes flat. Style suggestions in particular reflect one register. If you write fiction, dialogue, or in a variety of English the tool was not tuned for, expect a lot of false positives — and note that most checkers are tuned to US English by default.
