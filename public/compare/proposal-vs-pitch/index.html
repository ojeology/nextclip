Which format do I need?

Proposal vs pitch vs query letter

Three ways of asking for a yes. They differ in length, in what they promise, and in how much of the work you do before you send.

Intermediate Assumes you can already draft and revise a piece.

All three say: let me do this thing, and back me. The difference is how much of the thing already exists and how much detail the reader needs before deciding. A pitch is short and sells an idea. A proposal is long and specifies delivery. A query letter is a publishing-specific form with conventions so fixed that breaking them marks you as an outsider immediately.

Pitch

A short, specific idea aimed at one editor or client.

Typical length: 150–400 words. An email, not an attachment.

Who reads it: A commissioning editor with a full inbox

Voice: Direct and confident. No preamble, no apology.

Shape: The idea in one or two sentences, why now, why this outlet, why you, length and timing.

Use it when: Selling an article, a feature or a column to a publication.

Proposal

A detailed plan someone is being asked to fund or approve.

Typical length: 2–50 pages depending on the money involved

Who reads it: A committee, a client or a funder comparing you against alternatives

Voice: Formal, structured, evidenced. Costed.

Shape: Problem, approach, deliverables, timeline, budget, team, risks, evaluation.

Use it when: Grants, consultancy work, book proposals for non-fiction, internal projects.

Query letter

A one-page approach to a literary agent about a finished book.

Typical length: 250–400 words. One page, no exceptions.

Who reads it: An agent reading a hundred a week and looking for a reason to stop

Voice: Professional and controlled. The hook does the work.

Shape: Hook, a short blurb like back-cover copy, title, word count and genre, a brief bio, thank you.

Use it when: Approaching literary agents or publishers with a novel or memoir.

Decide quickly

Which one do you actually need?

  • Selling an article to a magazine → pitch.
  • Asking for money against a plan → proposal.
  • Approaching a literary agent → query letter.
  • The work does not exist yet and shouldn't → pitch or proposal.
  • The work must be finished before you send → query letter, for fiction and memoir. Agents will ask for the manuscript and you must have it.
  • There is a budget → proposal.
  • There is a submission portal with fixed fields → follow the portal, and keep your pitch or query as the raw material.

Common confusions

The questions that come up every time.

Can I pitch a novel?

Not to a magazine. Novels go to agents by query letter, and the manuscript must be complete if it is fiction or memoir. Non-fiction is the exception: it sells on a proposal and sample chapters, because the book's value is the argument and your authority to make it.

How long before I follow up?

Pitches: two weeks, once, in the same thread, two lines. Queries: whatever the agent's site states, and never sooner. Proposals: whatever the call specified. Following up early is the most common self-inflicted wound in all three forms.

Do I need clips or credits?

It helps and it is not a gate. Editors commission unpublished writers all the time when the idea is right and the pitch shows they can write — the pitch itself is a writing sample, which is why a sloppy one ends the conversation regardless of your credits.
