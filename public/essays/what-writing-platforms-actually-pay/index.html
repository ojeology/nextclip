What writing platforms actually pay — the numbers, not the pitches

Every platform publishes a percentage. Almost none publishes an average. The gap between those two numbers is where writers lose years.

By Ibrahim Sodiq · 2026-09-06

← All essays

Every platform that pays writers advertises a percentage. Substack says 90%. Ream says 90%. Polymemo says 70%. Vocal quotes a rate per thousand reads.

Percentages are the wrong number. A percentage of nothing is nothing, and on most of these platforms the thing being divided is very small. What you want is the average, and almost nobody publishes it.

I went looking for the arithmetic across seven platforms. Here is what is actually documented.

The one platform that publishes an average

Ream Stories states on its own blog that it is trusted by 15,000+ authors, reaching 140,000+ readers, with over $1.3 million earned by creators on Ream each year.

That is a good-faith disclosure, and it is more than any competitor offers. It also divides.

$1.3 million ÷ 15,000 authors ≈ $87 per author per year.

About $7.25 a month, on average, across everyone on the platform.

Ream's own marketing describes income bands of $50–300/month for early-stage authors, $500–3,000 growing, and $3,000–10,000+ established. Those bands are probably accurate for the authors in them. But they describe a small top cohort. The average tells you what happens to everybody else, and the average is $87 a year.

This is not a criticism of Ream — it is the most transparent platform in this entire comparison, and publishing a figure that divides this way takes some nerve. It is a criticism of every other platform that publishes bands and never publishes a total.

Substack: the median is zero

Substack is the biggest success story in the category, and its top-line numbers are real. The platform has reported roughly $450 million in gross writer earnings for 2025, up from about $370 million in 2024, with paid subscriptions passing 8.4 million in early 2026 and nearly 100,000 publications earning money globally — almost 30,000 of them outside the United States.

Then the distribution.

Independent analyses of the creator base put median annual earnings for writers who monetise at around $4,000, with the top quartile near $16,000. But roughly 90% of Substack newsletters never turn on paid subscriptions at all. Include them, and the true median across all newsletters is zero.

Among those who do charge, most take home under $1,000 a month, and nearly half earned under $500 in a year.

Substack takes 10%, Stripe about 2.9% + $0.30, so you keep roughly 87%. That share is genuinely good. The problem was never the percentage — it is that free-to-paid conversion typically runs 3–10%, so 500 paying readers usually means a free list of 5,000–15,000, and reaching that from cold takes most writers two to four years.

Medium: $10 to get paid, $5 to be allowed to earn

Medium's terms are precise and worth knowing.

From its own help documentation: minimum payout $10 USD, payouts initiated by the 10th of each month for the previous month, then a further 5–7 business days to reach your bank. Balances under $10 roll over. A connected Stripe account and verified taxpayer information are required.

The part that changes the calculation: you must be a paying member at $5/month to join the Partner Program. That is $60 a year for the right to earn.

Reported earnings from writers, across multiple accounts: new writers see $0.01 to $10 a month. Consistent non-viral writers land around $50–200 a month. Crossing $500 a month puts you in a small top tier.

So a typical outcome is $600–2,400 a year against a $60 annual cost. Positive, but the membership fee is a real hurdle for anyone earning at the bottom of that range.

Vocal: do the division before you start

Vocal publishes its rates openly, which is to its credit:

FreeVocal+ ($9.99/mo)
Per 1,000 reads$3.80$6.00
Minimum withdrawal$35$20
Withdrawal fee7%2.9%

At $3.80 per thousand, $35 requires roughly 9,200 reads. If your pieces average 100 reads, that is about 92 articles before your first withdrawal.

Vocal is a real company — Creatd, Inc., publicly traded — and it does pay. There are also repeated, credible complaints on Trustpilot about account suspensions with accrued balances withheld. If you use it, withdraw at the threshold rather than letting a balance build.

Polymemo: 49 cents on the dollar

The newest entrant, and the one with the widest gap between its coverage and its terms.

Polymemo pays authors 70% of viewing revenue. It also redeems points at a 70% rate — 5,000 points, worth $50 at its own stated 1pt = $0.01, cash out at $35.

Compounded, about 49 cents of every reader dollar reaches the author as cash. Readers must spend roughly $71.43 on your work for you to withdraw $35.

There is also no independent evidence that anyone has been paid — the platform is four months old. [Full breakdown here](/essays/polymemo-review-the-49-percent/).

Publish0x and Stacker News: honest, tiny

Publish0x is unusual and worth respecting: tips come from a sponsor-funded pool, so readers pay nothing out of pocket. Minimum payout is $0.50, paid in crypto. Realistic earnings for regular writers are documented at $5–10 a month.

Stacker News pays in bitcoin over Lightning. Its FAQ publishes the split plainly: a 100-sat zap pays 70 to the writer, 21 to the territory founder, 9 to the rewards pool. Earnings are unmeasured, and the most candid assessment I found came from a working writer on the platform itself, describing zap income as "peanuts/cherries on top, compared to the major revenue flow of writing" — which for them came from editors commissioning work.

A single commissioned feature can out-earn a year of average platform income. That is not a close call, and it is the finding that should change what you do on Monday.

Why the gap is so large

Three structural reasons, and none of them is that platform writers are worse.

Platforms pay from a pool; editors pay from a budget. A commissioning editor has money allocated before you pitch. A platform pays you a slice of whatever readers happened to spend.

Platforms need you to bring the audience anyway. Every honest account of Substack, Medium, Vocal and Stacker News says the same thing: the platform does not find readers for you. If you must bring the audience regardless, the question becomes what each route pays for the audience you brought — and commissions pay more per piece.

Distribution is brutally uneven everywhere. On Substack the top 10% capture more than 60% of all reader payments. Ream's $87 average sits under bands reaching $10,000/month. Averages are low because a few people earn most of it.

What to do with this

Do not pick a platform as your income plan. Pick markets that commission, and use a platform as the place your work lives between commissions. BRYME tracks [138 verified paying publications](/writing/) with the rate stated and sourced on every listing.

If you do use a platform, pick for the asset, not the rate. Substack's real value is an exportable email list you keep if the platform dies — which, as the [collapse of onchain publishing](/essays/onchain-publishing-for-writers-is-over/) showed, is not hypothetical.

Divide before you commit. Any platform quoting a percentage without a total is telling you half the story. Ask what the average author earns. If they will not say, assume it resembles $87 a year.

Specialise instead. The single largest documented lever in writing income is not platform choice — it is subject. The [highest-paying niches](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/high-paying-writing-niches/) show four-figure differences per day rate that no platform percentage can match.
