How to write a personal statement for university

What admissions tutors are actually reading for, how to open without a cliché, and how to fit it into the character limit.

Beginner Start here — assumes no prior knowledge.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Academic writing guides

An admissions tutor may read three hundred of these for one course. They are not looking for the best-written statement. They are looking for evidence that you are genuinely interested in the subject and capable of studying it at that level — and they can establish that in about ninety seconds.

Which means the question is not "how do I stand out?" but "how do I show, quickly and specifically, that I have actually engaged with this subject?"

The one thing that separates good from bad

Everyone writes "I have always been passionate about psychology". Nobody can disprove it and it therefore carries no information.

What carries information is what you did about it. Not the feeling — the evidence. You read something outside the syllabus and it changed how you saw the topic. You tried something and it did not work. You noticed a problem in your own town and went looking for why. That is the currency. A statement made entirely of evidence needs no adjectives at all.

The structure

Why this subject. Not your childhood. The intellectual reason, ideally through something specific. Get to it in the first two sentences.

What you have done about it — academically. Reading beyond the syllabus, projects, essays, competitions, online courses, a coursework question that opened something up. For each one, say what you took from it. Naming a book proves you can type a title; saying what it changed proves you read it.

What you have done about it — beyond the classroom. Work experience, volunteering, a job, something you built or ran. Relevance is what matters, and relevance can be indirect if you explain the link: shift work taught you to work when tired, which is not nothing.

Other activities, briefly. Sport, music, responsibility. One short paragraph at most. This section is much less important than applicants believe.

Why you are ready. What you want to explore at degree level. Specific enough to show you know what the course contains.

Roughly 75–80% subject, 20–25% everything else. The commonest structural error is inverting that.

The opening

Do not open with a quotation. Do not open with a dictionary definition. Do not open with "From a young age…" or "Ever since I can remember…". Tutors have a documented aversion to all four, and they appear in a startling proportion of statements.

Open with the thing itself. What you noticed, what you read, what you could not work out. "I spent a summer trying to understand why the market women in my mother's town all set prices within an hour of each other, and eventually found the phrase for it: tacit collusion." That is a first line that has already proved something.

The limit

UCAS allows 4,000 characters and 47 lines, whichever you hit first — spaces count. Most other systems set a word count. Write long, then cut, and cut adverbs, hedges and any sentence that would be true of any applicant.

For UCAS: one statement goes to every course you apply to. Do not name a university in it. If your courses are very different, you have a real problem — the statement can only be about what they share.

Common mistakes

Claiming passion instead of demonstrating interest. Listing books without saying what you took from them. Spending half the statement on sport. Explaining that a subject is "important in today's world". Exaggerating anything — you may be interviewed on it. Copying phrasing from an online example, which is checked for similarity and does get flagged. Leaving it to the week of the deadline.

Practical tips

  • Write the middle first. The opening is easiest once you know what you are opening.
  • For every claim, ask "how would a reader know that is true?" and put the answer in.
  • Read it aloud to someone who does not do your subject; ask them what your interest actually is.
  • If mitigating circumstances affected your grades, they usually belong in the reference, not here — check with your teacher.
  • Draft it in a plain text editor and paste in last. Curly quotes and formatting break character counts.

Regional variation

The UCAS personal statement (4,000 characters, one statement for every course, no university named) is a UK-specific form. US application essays are prompt-driven and can be personal narratives; European motivation letters must name the programme and its staff. See how conventions differ by country →
