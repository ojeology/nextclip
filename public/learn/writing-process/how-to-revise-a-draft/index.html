How to revise a draft

Turn a rough first draft into a sharper, clearer piece by revising big to small.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Writing process guides

Revision is where most good writing is actually made. A first draft is raw material; the revision is the craft. The mistake most writers make is revising at the wrong scale — fixing commas before fixing the structure.

What it is

Revision is the stage where you improve what the piece says, before you polish how it reads. It happens big-to-small: structure, then section, then paragraph, then sentence.

Why it matters

Almost no one's first draft is their best work. Revision is cheap and gives the biggest quality gains per minute spent. Editing typos in a piece that has the wrong structure is wasted effort.

Step-by-step guide

Let it cool. Step away for a while, or at least a few minutes, so you can see it with fresh eyes. Distance removes the love of your own words.

Read for the big picture first. Does the whole piece do what it sets out to do? Is the main point clear? Cut sections that do not earn their place and move the ones that should come earlier.

Check the order. Does each section lead naturally to the next? Is the strongest material positioned where it works best?

Check the opening and closing. Does the introduction make the reader want to continue? Does the conclusion do justice to the piece rather than just repeat it?

Then move to paragraph level. Does each paragraph have one job? Split or merge as needed.

Then sentences. Remove repetition, tighten wordy phrases, and vary length.

Read it aloud. Your ear catches awkwardness your eye misses. Mark anything you stumble over.

Common mistakes

Editing typos before fixing the structure. Rewriting from scratch instead of working with what is there. Cutting a section you like even though it does not belong. Stopping at the first sentence-level fix without rereading the whole piece.

Practical tips

  • Revise big-to-small and never in reverse.
  • Delete words that repeat the same idea twice.
  • When you cut, save the cut in a separate note; you may use it later.
  • Ask a trusted reader to say what confused them.
