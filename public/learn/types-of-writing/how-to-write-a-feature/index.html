Types of writing

How to write a feature

A longer, story-driven article that explores a subject in depth with human voices.

Advanced For longer or professional projects with higher stakes.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Types of writing guides

A feature is a longer piece of journalism that goes deeper than a news article. Instead of reporting facts fast, it explores a subject through people, scenes and a narrative — and it is written to be read, not just skimmed.

What it is

A feature is a substantial article, usually in a magazine or long-form section, that takes one subject and investigates it through reporting, characters and a story. It can be a profile, a trend story, a travel piece, an explainer, or a personal narrative.

How to write it

Find a person or a story to hang it on. A feature is rarely about an abstract idea; it is about people who embody it. Find a character whose experience carries the theme.

Report deeply, then narrow. Gather more than you need — interviews, scenes, facts. Then decide the core: the one thing you want the reader to understand or feel.

Open on a scene, not a summary. A strong feature opens with a moment — a person in a place, doing something. Show, then pull back to explain.

Build a narrative arc. A feature has a beginning (a scene or question), a middle (development, tension, discovery) and an end (a turn, a resolution, a resonance). It is a story, not a list.

Weave in voices. Real quotes make a feature live. Let your sources speak, and attribute accurately.

Include context and facts. The narrative needs substance — facts, figures and background — woven in so the reader learns while they read.

Land a strong close. The final line should resonate, echoing the opening or the core insight. It should not just stop.

How to structure it

  1. The opening scene — the reader is placed in a moment.
  2. The premise — what the piece is about, and why it matters.
  3. The development — reporting, voices and scenes that build the case.
  4. A turn or insight — a shift, discovery or deepening.
  5. The close — a line that lands and stays with the reader.

Common mistakes

Writing a list of facts instead of a story. Starting with a generic introduction. Not going deep enough. Too many sources, no central character. An ending that just stops.

Practical tips

  • Find the strongest scene you witnessed or can picture, and start there.
  • Let one person's story be the thread the reader follows.
  • Read the whole thing aloud for pacing.
