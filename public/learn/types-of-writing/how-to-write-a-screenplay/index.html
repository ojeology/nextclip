Types of writing

How to write a screenplay

Write a script that a reader can follow — scene by scene, in standard format, with the visual and spoken told.

Advanced For longer or professional projects with higher stakes.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Types of writing guides

A screenplay is written to be seen. Unlike prose, it does not describe interior thoughts — it shows what a camera and an actor can physically present, in the standard format that readers and producers expect.

What it is

A screenplay tells a story through action and dialogue, formatted in scenes. It is the blueprint for a film or show; the writing is spare, visual, and designed to move fast.

Why it matters

A screenplay is a practical document. A reader (an agent, producer, or director) needs to see the story and the scene structure at a glance. Professional format is a signal that you know the craft.

How to write it

Use standard screenplay format. A title page, then scenes. Each scene has a scene heading (INT or EXT, the location, and the time of day), followed by action lines (in present tense), then character names above their dialogue, with optional parentheticals (small delivery notes) and transitions (like CUT TO:).

Write action visually, not internally. Show what is seen. "She grips the cup so hard her knuckles whiten" — not "she is nervous." Do not tell the reader what a character is thinking.

Keep action lines short. Screenplays are read fast; a heavy block of description slows a reader down. Use short, present-tense, visual lines.

Let dialogue carry the scene. Use the dialogue guide — line by character, with subtext. Characters in a script often say one thing and mean another.

Build the scene. A scene has a purpose: it advances the story, reveals character, or raises the stakes. If a scene does none of these, cut it.

Write for the page and the screen. The reader is imagining the film. Paint clear pictures, make the action readable, and keep the story moving.

Know the conventional length. A feature is typically around 90–120 pages (roughly one page per minute of screen time). Shorts and episodes differ.

Example of form

INT. KITCHEN — DAY

MAYA grips the edge of the counter. A phone buzzes on the table.

MAYA (quietly) You can't keep doing this.

SAM I'm not the one who—

MAYA You're the one who left.

CUT TO:

Common mistakes

Writing it like a novel with interior monologue. Over-describing action in long blocks. Dialogue that sounds like an essay. Ignoring standard format. Scenes that do not advance anything. No clear story conflict.

Practical tips

  • Read one page of your script aloud; it should sound like a film.
  • Keep action lines to a few lines each.
  • Enter a scene late and leave early.
  • Study the format before writing — it matters to readers.
