PDF and document tools for freelance writers — most writers need three, not thirteen

Marking up edits, signing contracts, and converting formats are the only PDF jobs a writing business actually has. The honest tool list, the privacy rule for unpublished manuscripts, and what e-signatures are legally worth.

Beginner Start here — assumes no prior knowledge.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Freelance & paid writing guides

BRYME has not run these tools as a paying customer over months, so this is not a ranked "best" list. It is the mapping from the three document jobs a freelance writing business actually has to the tools that do them — including the free ones most writers already own and don't use.

Tool-roundup sites list fifteen PDF editors because fifteen PDF editors exist. A working writer has exactly three recurring document jobs: marking up documents other people send, signing things, and converting formats. Buy for those three jobs and ignore the rest of the table.

Job one: markup and editing

Editors send PDFs with queries; you return tracked responses. Clients send page proofs; you flag typos. This is annotation, and it is nearly free everywhere:

  • macOS Preview and Windows' built-in tools handle highlighting, notes, form-filling and page reordering for nothing. A plurality of writers need nothing more, ever.
  • Adobe Acrobat is the reference standard for true editing — changing existing text in place, OCR on scans, proper redaction. It is also subscription-priced like a professional tool: roughly $13–15/month (Standard) to $20–24/month (Pro) on annual billing, and notably more month-to-month. Pay that when a client's workflow demands it, not because a roundup implied PDFs are hard.
  • PDF-XChange Editor (Windows, perpetual licence around $62–79) and PDF Expert (Mac/iPad, about $80/year or $140 lifetime) are the serious one-time-cost alternatives.
  • Free when the privacy trade is acceptable: PDF24 (offline, Windows, genuinely free) and Sejda's free tier (three tasks per hour, files auto-deleted). Cloud tools like iLovePDF and Smallpdf (free tiers, Pro roughly $7 and $12/month) are convenient — see the privacy rule below before uploading manuscripts to them.

Job two: signing

Contracts are the writer's daily PDF: the [contract checklist](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/writing-contracts-what-to-check/) matters more than the signing tool, but the signature still needs to happen.

  • An electronic signature is legally binding in the US (ESIGN Act) and UK/EU (eIDAS) for ordinary commercial contracts — a typed name in a clear intent-to-sign flow counts. Either party can demand more formality, so match the tool to the counterparty.
  • Free tiers that genuinely work: PandaDoc's free plan covers about 60 documents a year — for most freelance writers, that is every contract they sign. DocuSign's personal plan (~$11/month) exists mainly for counterparties whose legal departments expect the brand.
  • The zero-cost route: Preview and Edge both insert a signature image into a PDF, and "sign, date, email the PDF back" remains a fully executed contract. Reserve paid e-signature for clients who route through procurement.

Job three: converting

Manuscript to PDF for a pitch packet; client DOCX to PDF for an invoice; PDF text back into editable form when a client lost the source file. In order of honesty:

  1. Word processors export PDF natively — the writer's default conversion is already installed.
  2. Pandoc (free) converts between nearly every text format and is the writer's quiet superpower.
  3. OCR on scans is the one job worth paying for — Acrobat does it best; free tools do it adequately when the scan is clean.

The privacy rule for unpublished work

Cloud PDF tools upload your files to their servers to process them. For merged invoices that's nothing; for an unpublished manuscript, a source's confidential documents, or anything covered by an NDA, it is a decision someone should make deliberately. This site's rule:

  • NDA'd, embargoed or pre-publication material stays on your machine — offline tools (Preview, PDF24, desktop Acrobat, Pandoc).
  • Cloud tools get the disposable paperwork — invoices, signed releases, public documents.

It costs nothing and it is the kind of operational hygiene that sophisticated clients notice. The same logic drives the [project and client tooling choices](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/project-and-client-tools-for-writers/): convenience on public work, custody on private work.

What to do this month

  1. Open your OS's built-in annotation tools and actually try them on the last PDF an editor sent. If they cover you, job one is done at $0.
  2. Set up one free e-signature flow (PandaDoc free tier, or your signature image in Preview) so contracts never wait on ceremony.
  3. Learn Pandoc in an afternoon, or accept you'll never need it — there is no middle case.
  4. Write your own two-line privacy rule (what stays local, what may touch the cloud) and keep it next to your [invoice workflow](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/how-to-invoice-as-a-writer/).
  5. Only buy Acrobat or a paid editor when a specific client requirement names it. Subscription PDF tools are the most commonly auto-renewed, least-used line in a writer's software budget.

Pricing reflects published vendor information as of 6 September 2026 (Acrobat Standard ~$13–15/mo, Pro ~$20–24/mo on annual billing; PDF-XChange ~$62–79 perpetual; PDF Expert ~$80/yr or $140 lifetime; Smallpdf Pro ~$12/mo; iLovePDF Pro ~$7/mo; Sejda free 3 tasks/hour; PandaDoc free ~60 docs/yr; DocuSign Personal ~$11/mo). Pricing in this category is deliberately confusing — verify at checkout. Not a ranked list; no affiliate relationships.
