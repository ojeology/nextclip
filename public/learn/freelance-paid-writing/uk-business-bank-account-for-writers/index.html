UK business bank accounts for writers — and the rule that catches sole traders

A UK sole trader has no legal duty to hold a business account. But most personal-account terms prohibit business use, which is the part nobody mentions.

Beginner Start here — assumes no prior knowledge.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

General information, not financial advice. Bank terms change constantly. Check the provider's current terms before opening anything.

The UK answer differs from the US answer in one specific and important way, and almost no comparison article mentions it.

No legal duty — but read your personal account terms

A UK sole trader is not legally required to hold a separate business bank account. You and the business are the same legal person, and HMRC does not mandate separation.

But most UK personal current account terms explicitly prohibit business use. That is a contractual matter between you and your bank, not a tax matter, and banks do enforce it — usually by writing to you and asking you to move, occasionally by closing the account.

So the practical UK position is:

  • Legally you can use a personal account
  • Contractually your bank probably says you cannot
  • Practically many writers use a second personal account and are never troubled, especially at low volumes

The cleanest answer, if you are earning consistently, is a proper business account. Several are free.

If you have formed a limited company, this is not optional. The company's money is legally not yours, and a company must have its own account.

What actually matters for a writer

Free is genuinely available. Several UK digital banks offer free business accounts for sole traders with no monthly fee and no minimum balance. High-street business accounts typically offer an introductory free period and then charge a monthly fee.

Check whether they accept sole traders, not just limited companies. Some providers are company-only, which is the same trap as Mercury in the US.

Receiving foreign payments is the real cost. If US, Canadian or Australian publications pay you, the exchange rate and receiving fee will dwarf any monthly account fee. Ask specifically:

  • What is charged on an incoming international payment?
  • What exchange rate is applied, and what is the margin over mid-market?
  • Can I hold or receive USD, EUR or CAD directly?

For most UK writers with international income, this single question matters more than everything else in the comparison — see [payment platforms for writers](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/payment-platforms-for-writers/).

Accounting integration and Making Tax Digital. MTD for Income Tax applies from April 2026 to self-employment income over £50,000, from April 2027 over £30,000, and from April 2028 over £20,000. If you are heading toward those thresholds, an account that feeds cleanly into MTD-compatible software saves real time. See [UK tax for writers](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/uk-tax-for-freelance-writers/).

Cash and cheques. Almost irrelevant for writers, and worth knowing the digital banks handle them poorly or not at all. If a magazine still pays by cheque, check before you switch.

What writers do not need

A branch. Nothing about invoicing a magazine requires one. Card machines or merchant services. You are invoicing, not selling over a counter. An overdraft or lending facility. A lumpy freelance income is exactly the wrong thing to smooth with borrowing. Bookkeeping bundled at a premium. Free tools already cover a writer's needs — see [accounting software](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/accounting-software-for-writers/).

Deliberately not naming a "best" account

Because UK bank pricing and terms change several times a year, and because the same providers publish different offers by month and by customer segment. Any article naming a winner is out of date within a quarter — the same pattern documented in [invoicing software](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/invoicing-software-for-writers/), where five sources quoted six different prices for one product.

Compare on the criteria above, on the provider's own current page, from your own circumstances.

The honest recommendation

Earning under a few thousand a year: a second personal account gives you the tax-time benefit at zero cost, with the caveat about account terms above.

Earning consistently as a sole trader: open a free business account that accepts sole traders. There is no reason to pay a monthly fee.

Limited company: you need a business account, and the free digital options are a sensible default.

Significant income from abroad: choose on FX and receiving fees, not the headline monthly cost.

Check what you actually earn first with the [rate calculator](/tools/freelance-rate-calculator/).
