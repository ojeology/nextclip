Getting paid across borders — payment platforms for writers

Stripe covers 46 countries. PayPal personal accounts in Nigeria cannot receive. Here is what actually reaches a writer's bank account, and where each rail stops.

Intermediate Assumes you can already draft and revise a piece.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Freelance & paid writing guides

Most advice about getting paid is written from inside a country where getting paid works. Read it from Lagos, Manila or Nairobi and you find out the hard way that the button does not work for you.

This is the version with the country lists attached.

The core problem, in one line

A platform being available in your country and a platform being able to pay you are different things, and almost every "best payment platform" article conflates them.

Stripe is the clearest example. Nigeria is in what Stripe calls its Extended Network, through its subsidiary Paystack. That means a business can accept payments from Nigerian customers via Stripe. It does not mean a Nigerian can receive a Stripe payout. Two different products, one name.

Where each rail actually stops

Stripe — 46 countries for payouts

The clearest public evidence of Stripe's real payout coverage comes from a platform that publishes its list. Ream Stories, a subscription platform for fiction authors, offers a "Direct" option where the author connects their own Stripe account. Ream publishes that list: 46 countries.

Australia · Austria · Belgium · Brazil · Bulgaria · Canada · Croatia · Cyprus · Czech Republic · Denmark · Estonia · Finland · France · Germany · Gibraltar · Greece · Hong Kong · Hungary · Ireland · Italy · Latvia · Liechtenstein · Lithuania · Luxembourg · Malaysia · Malta · Mexico · Netherlands · New Zealand · Norway · Poland · Portugal · Romania · Singapore · Slovakia · Slovenia · Spain · Sweden · Switzerland · Thailand · UAE · United Kingdom · United States

If your country is not on that list, every Stripe-based platform is closed to you — and that includes Substack, Medium and most subscription tools. India, Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa, the Philippines and most of Latin America outside Mexico and Brazil are all absent.

Stripe also runs a lighter product, Stripe Express, used by platforms to pay contributors. Its coverage is wider and platforms enable it country by country. That is why some writers report being paid by Vocal but not by Substack — different rails, same company.

PayPal — check "receive", not "available"

PayPal is available almost everywhere. Receiving is the constrained part.

As of 2026, multiple current sources report that standard personal PayPal accounts opened in Nigeria are send-only — they can pay for goods and services abroad but cannot receive personal payments or withdraw a balance to a Nigerian bank. Some Nigerian outlets report a 2026 partnership that changes this. The evidence conflicts, and BRYME will not state it either way. Verify against PayPal's own country functionality page before relying on it.

Two further distinctions worth knowing:

PayPal Payouts API is not consumer PayPal. It is a mass-payment product with its own eligibility. Polymemo, for instance, redeems points through Payouts — so "I have a PayPal account" does not settle whether you can be paid.

The VPN or foreign-address workaround is a trap. It breaches PayPal's user agreement, their fraud systems flag mismatched IPs and documents — usually exactly when a large payment arrives — and a limited account means funds frozen for 180 days. Do not.

Wise, Payoneer and virtual USD accounts

These solve a different problem: they give you foreign account details that a payer can send to using local rails.

  • Payoneer integrates with many platforms directly and supports Nigerian users. Watch the conversion spread.
  • Wise works well where the sender initiates the transfer, and is transparent about the mid-market rate.
  • Grey and similar fintechs issue USD, GBP and EUR virtual accounts aimed at African freelancers, converting to local currency.

The trade-off is always the same: you gain access, you pay a spread. On a £150 magazine fee, a 3–4% combined conversion and receiving cost is £4.50–6. On a £2,000 month it is £60–80, and worth optimising.

Bank transfer — still the most common way writers are paid

This is what the guides forget. Magazines mostly pay by bank transfer against an invoice, not through a platform. Pellicle, Briarpatch, Geist, The Fiction Desk — none of these need you to have Stripe.

That single fact reorders the advice: if your income is commissions from publications, the payment-rail problem is much smaller than it looks. It becomes acute only when you try to monetise an audience directly through a platform.

Bitcoin Lightning — the permissionless outlier

Worth knowing about even if you never use it. Platforms like Stacker News pay in bitcoin over the Lightning Network, which has no country list at all — nothing in the payout path checks where you live.

Earnings there are very small, and local tax and regulatory treatment of bitcoin is your responsibility. But it is the one working demonstration that the payout wall is a business decision rather than a technical necessity.

What to do, by situation

If you are in one of the 46 Stripe countries: you have no access problem. Optimise for fees, not availability.

If you are not: do not build your plan around Substack or any Stripe-based subscription product. Two better routes:

  1. Take commissions from publications that pay by bank transfer. BRYME lists [142 verified paying publications](/writing/), most of which invoice and pay by transfer. This is the highest-value route and it sidesteps the wall entirely.
  2. Open a virtual USD or GBP account with Payoneer, Wise or Grey, and give those details to payers.

Before you accept any commission, confirm three things in writing: the fee, the currency, and how they will send it. The third is the one writers forget and then spend two months chasing.
