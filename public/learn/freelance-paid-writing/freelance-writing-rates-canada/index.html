Freelance writing rates in Canada — the dollar-a-word that never moved

PWAC's guidelines say $1–$2 a word for consumer magazines. Writers report 35c to 99c. And rates have barely moved since the 1980s. Here is the full picture.

Intermediate Assumes you can already draft and revise a piece.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Freelance & paid writing guides

Canada has the bleakest and most useful rate data of any market BRYME has looked at, because Canadian journalism organisations have been honest about something the industry elsewhere avoids saying out loud.

The sentence that frames the whole market

From the Canadian Journalism Foundation, reporting on freelance earnings:

"Freelance rates have not increased since the 1980s, when experienced writers could earn one dollar per word from the highest-paying consumer magazines."

A dollar a word was the aspiration forty years ago. It is still the aspiration. Adjusted for inflation, the real rate has collapsed.

What the guidelines say

The Canadian Freelance Guild publishes rate guidelines, built on fee data collected from PWAC members across Canada and from industries that regularly hire writers.

Online and website writing$1.00–$3.00$60–$100/hr

The Guild is careful to say these are general guidelines only, that rates vary widely by city and experience, and that the exact fee is negotiated per assignment.

What writers actually report earning

PWAC's own reporting puts the reality much lower: most writers earn about 99 cents a word at the high end of the scale and 35 cents at the low end.

So the guideline floor for consumer magazines — $1.00 — is roughly the ceiling most working Canadian freelancers actually reach.

One further finding worth stating rather than skipping: PWAC's report and independent survey work both find that women freelance writers are paid less than men, and that the lowest rates are consistently paid to women, even as average lowest rates rise overall.

What Canadian publications actually pay

BRYME has verified 17 Canadian publications — more than any market except the US and UK, because Canadian magazines publish their rates more openly than anyone else. That openness is largely a product of arts-council funding norms.

Among those publishing a figure, the median is C$60 a piece, from C$10 to C$500.

Note The Walrus reaching the historic dollar-a-word for features, and Pulp Literature at five to eight cents. Same country, same year, a twelve-to-twenty-fold spread.

The three markets, and they behave differently

Commercial and government work genuinely pays the guideline rates. Government writing at $1–$3 a word is the highest-paying category in the Guild's schedule and the least discussed.

Consumer journalism aspires to a dollar a word and mostly does not reach it.

Literary magazines pay from grant budgets. C$50–C$100 is normal and is not a negotiating position.

What to do with this

Chase the categories nobody writes about. Government, corporate and technical writing at $1–$3 a word are in the Guild's own schedule and attract far less competition than consumer magazines paying half that.

Use The Tyee's structure as a model. A day rate of C$300 for a 1–3 day assignment is clearer and safer than a per-word quote, because it prices your time rather than your output. See [per word, day rate, or project fee](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/per-word-day-rate-or-project-fee/).

Know when nationality gates apply. The New Quarterly publishes Canadian writers exclusively. Geist prioritises Canadians, permanent residents, or a strong Canadian connection. Both are on the [Canada page](/writing-opportunities/canada/) with the rule stated.

Cite the Guild schedule when negotiating. It exists to be quoted, and most editors have seen it.

And know your floor. With a market where the realistic ceiling is around a dollar a word, working out what you actually need per hour matters more here than anywhere — use the [rate calculator](/tools/freelance-rate-calculator/) before accepting.

The honest summary

Canada publishes better rate guidance than almost anywhere and pays less than that guidance suggests. The useful response is not despair, it is targeting: the categories where the guideline rate is actually paid are government, corporate and technical work, not the consumer magazines writers most want to be in.
