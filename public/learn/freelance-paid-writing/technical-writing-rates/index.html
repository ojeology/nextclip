Freelance & paid writing

What technical writing pays

Technical writing rates from the EFA 2026 survey, plus how API, SaaS and compliance documentation differ in pay.

Intermediate Assumes you can already draft and revise a piece.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Freelance & paid writing guides

Technical writing sits just below medical in the rate tables and has a much lower barrier to entry. If you can read documentation, follow a process and write clearly, this is the most accessible of the high-paying niches.

The survey figures

Editorial Freelancers Association, 2026 Rate Chart — over 1,100 respondents, surveyed November 2025 to mid-January 2026 for rates charged in 2025:

For context in the same chart: blog posts run 25¢–45¢ per word. Technical writing pays roughly two to four times that.

Note the formatting row. It pays $69–$100/hour despite being the least writerly task on the list — because it requires knowing the toolchain.

How the sub-niches split

Published 2026 market analyses of freelance technical writing report a consistent ordering, though these are market surveys rather than association data, so treat them as directional:

SpecialismTypical hourly
API and developer documentation$80–$175
Medical and pharmaceutical technical writing$90–$200
Cybersecurity and compliance$85–$160
Software and SaaS documentation$60–$130
Manufacturing and engineering$55–$120
General how-to and knowledge base$30–$60

And by experience: entry-level (0–2 years) $30–$60, mid-level (2–5) $60–$100, senior (5+) $100–$175, specialised $120–$250.

The gap between "general how-to" at $30 and "API documentation" at $175 is the whole argument for specialising. It is the same core skill applied to a subject with a smaller supply of competent writers.

Why this niche is unusually reachable

Three reasons technical writing is the easiest high-rate niche to break into:

The subject matter is public. Every API you would ever document has published docs you can read. Every compliance framework is available. There is no gatekeeper on the knowledge.

Your sample can be unsolicited. Find a tool with poor documentation, rewrite one page properly, and publish it. That is a portfolio piece and a pitch in one. Nobody can do the equivalent in medical regulatory writing.

Clarity is the product, and it is measurable. A good doc reduces support tickets. That is a business case you can make in a pitch, which most writing cannot.

What the work actually is

API and developer documentation — reference material, quickstarts, integration guides. The highest-paying corner. You need to read code, though usually not write it, and you must be able to run the thing you are documenting.

Knowledge base and help centre — task-based articles for end users. Highest volume, lowest rate, most consistent demand.

Compliance and policy documentation — procedures, controls, audit material. Growing fast, pays well, and rewards precision over style.

White papers and technical marketing — the crossover into copywriting, often the best-paid per hour because it is priced as marketing rather than documentation.

How to price it

Technical writing is usually priced by project or hour, not per word, because word count is a bad proxy for effort — a well-written API reference is short and took a long time.

A workable method:

  1. Establish your floor with the [freelance rate calculator](/tools/freelance-rate-calculator/).
  2. Position against the EFA's $75–$126/hour technical band.
  3. Scope the project in deliverables (pages, endpoints, articles), not words.
  4. Quote a project fee with a defined revision count, and state what a change of scope costs.

Per-word pricing actively penalises you here. The clearer your documentation gets, the shorter it becomes, and the less you would earn.

Getting the first job

Rewrite something badly documented and publish it. Pick an open-source tool with weak docs. Do one page properly. This is the single most effective portfolio move available in this niche.

Learn one toolchain. Markdown and Git are the baseline. Knowing a docs-as-code workflow puts you ahead of most general writers immediately.

Pitch the support team, not marketing. Support leads feel documentation gaps daily and can quantify them. They are a warmer audience than a marketing manager.

Read the style guides. Google and Microsoft both publish theirs publicly. Following one visibly signals professionalism before you say a word about rates.
