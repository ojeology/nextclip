Examples of writing

Example of a newsletter

A complete newsletter with the structure that gets read to the end.

Beginner Start here — assumes no prior knowledge.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Examples of writing guides

A newsletter is not an essay — it asks for a few minutes of a reader's attention and answers with something useful. The example below shows the structure that keeps a reader scrolling to the end.

The example newsletter

Subject: A better way to start your writing day

[Month] · The BRYME Letter · Issue [n]

Hello, and welcome back.

Writing is hard to start and easy to avoid. This week: one small fix for the blank page, a tool that does half the work, and a question to ponder.

One thing to try — start with a single sentence

Most writers do not need more willpower; they need a smaller first step. Instead of "I will write for an hour," try "I will write one sentence about the part I understand best." It is impossible to refuse, and once the cursor moves, the session is alive. (Tie-in to your writing routine guide.)

A tool worth knowing

The word counter on BRYME is a quiet way to keep moving — set a target, stop counting, and just write until the number says you are done. Try it the next time the page feels empty. (Link.)

A question for you

What is the one piece you keep meaning to send but have not? This is your sign.

Until next week — keep writing.

— Ibrahim · BRYME

Why this works

The subject line promises a benefit, not a name. "A better way to start your writing day" tells the reader what they get before they open it. A good subject line is specific and worth the tap.

It greets warmly and sets up the email in one line. The reader knows what this issue covers and why to keep reading.

It is one idea per section, clearly marked. "One thing to try," "A tool worth knowing," "A question for you." The reader can scan and pick what they want.

Each section is short and has a clear takeaway. It is not a lecture; it is a few useful nuggets. Short beats long for an email.

It ends on a simple, warm close and a sign-off — not a hard sell. A newsletter builds a relationship; the delivery is the point.

The structure to copy

  1. A subject line promising a benefit.
  2. A short greeting and a one-line promise.
  3. A few clearly-marked, useful sections (a tip, a tool, a question).
  4. Short, scannable content with a takeaway each.
  5. A warm close and a consistent sign-off.

How to adapt it

  • Write the subject line last — it should promise what the email delivers.
  • Keep each section to a few sentences.
  • Give the reader one clear action or takeaway per section.
  • Be consistent in tone and layout.

Common mistakes

A boring or vague subject line. A long introduction before the point. Too many sections, none of them useful. A hard sell in every issue. Going on and on — a newsletter is not a report.
