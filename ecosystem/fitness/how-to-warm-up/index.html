How to warm up (in five minutes, without rituals)

BRYME Fitness desk · reviewed 2026-09-09 · general information, not medical advice

In one line: What a warm-up actually does, the three-part pattern that covers every session, and where static stretching honestly belongs.

A warm-up is not a ceremony; it is a rehearsal. Done in five minutes, it raises your temperature, opens the joints you are about to use, and lets the first working set feel like the second. Skip it and sessions feel heavier, tighter and more accident-prone — that is the whole case, and it needs no mysticism.

The three-part pattern

One: raise. Two or three minutes of easy movement — marching on the spot, a slow walk building to a brisk one, gentle cycling. You are aiming for warmth and slightly deeper breath, not fatigue. Two: open. A minute of dynamic moves for the joints the session will use: leg swings front and side for legs days, arm circles and torso turns for upper-body days, hip circles always. Dynamic means moving through the range — not holding. Three: rehearse. Do the first exercise of your session at an easy scale: half-depth squats before squats, wall push-ups before floor push-ups, a slower first five minutes of the walk. This is the part beginners skip and athletes never do.

Where static stretching honestly fits

The long-hold stretches you picture when someone says “stretching” belong mostly after the session, on warm muscles — as a cool-down ritual, which is worthwhile for the wind-down even though its magic-injury-prevention reputation has been softened by research. Before exercise, long static holds can slightly reduce power output and do little to prevent injury; the moving version (part two above) is the better pre-session tool. Both have a place; the order is the point.

The honest minimum

For a brisk walk, the warm-up is the first five minutes walked gently — our walking plan bakes that in. For the strength sessions, use the full three-part pattern; it costs five minutes and buys a session that feels lighter from the first rep. General information, not medical advice — if a joint hurts during the warm-up itself, that is information worth respecting, not pushing through.
