The offside rule, explained like a friend would

BRYME Sport desk · reviewed 2026-09-09 · evergreen explainer — no odds, no tips, no invented facts

In one line: Position, timing and involvement: the three ideas that unlock football's most-argued law — and why assistants now let play breathe before flagging.

Offside confuses people for one reason: it is three rules wearing one shirt. Learn the three ideas and the arguments mostly evaporate.

Idea one: position

You are in an offside position if, at the moment a teammate plays the ball to you, any part of you that can legally score is closer to the opponent's goal line than both the ball and the second-last defender. Read that again slowly: the goalkeeper usually counts as one of the two defenders, so the working version is “level with the last defender or the ball is safe.” Level is onside — the benefit of the doubt is written into the law itself. Hands and arms do not count for either side; you cannot be offside on your arm and you cannot play a defender onside with theirs.

Idea two: timing

The judgement moment is when the pass is played, not when it arrives. A striker drifting beyond the defence is perfectly legal if they were behind the line when their teammate struck the ball. This is why offside arguments are really arguments about freeze-frames, and why television draws lines for a living.

Idea three: involvement

Being in an offside position is not an offence. It becomes one only when you get involved: interfering with play, interfering with an opponent (a defender's ability to play the ball — including being in their sightline), or gaining an advantage from that position, such as playing a rebound off the post. A player standing offside and silent while the ball goes elsewhere has done nothing wrong. This is the part casual watchers miss, and it is why assistant referees hold their flags: if the offside player never touches the ball, the flag should stay down and play should breathe.

The modern wrinkles, honestly dated

Two things make offside feel different from a decade ago. Semi-automated offside technology at the biggest competitions tracks the freeze-frame with sensors and draws the line for the officials — faster, and arguable in a new way (a toe's width, measured precisely). And interpretations get clarified over time: in 2023 top leagues agreed to flag only once a player actually touches the ball, formalising the “wait and see” practice. The core law is stable; the margins are engineered. When a decision confuses you, ask which of the three ideas is being argued — position, timing or involvement — and the argument usually names itself.
