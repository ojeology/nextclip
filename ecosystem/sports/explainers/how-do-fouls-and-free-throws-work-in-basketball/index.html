Basketball rules

How do fouls and free throws work in basketball?

The short answer

A foul is illegal contact with an opponent; the penalty depends on what the player was doing. Foul a shooter and he gets free throws; foul a non-shooter once your team is over the foul limit and he shoots too. Players foul out after six personal fouls in the NBA, or five under FIBA and college rules.

The distinction that matters is whether the player was shooting. Foul a player in the act of shooting and he goes to the line: two free throws for a two-point attempt, three for a three-point attempt. If the shot still goes in despite the foul, he gets one extra free throw — the 'and-one' that turns a good play into a three- or four-point possession. Foul a player who was not shooting and, below the team foul limit, the penalty is only a sideline inbound.

Team fouls change that calculation. Each quarter, once a team passes the limit — five fouls in an NBA quarter, four under FIBA and college rules — every subsequent defensive foul sends the opponent to the free-throw line even when nobody was shooting. This is the 'bonus', and it is why teams become cautious late in a quarter: the fifth foul in the last two minutes of an NBA quarter can put the other side on the line immediately, possession after possession.

Individual fouls accumulate as well. A player is disqualified after six personal fouls in the NBA and five under FIBA and college rules — 'fouling out' — and that limit is why stars in foul trouble sit on the bench in the first half of big games. Coaches manage it deliberately: a player with two early fouls is a liability, and protecting him is one of the most consequential decisions in a half.

Not all fouls are ordinary personal fouls. A technical foul is a non-contact offence — dissent, delay of game, too many timeouts, hanging on the rim — punished with a free throw and possession. A flagrant foul in the NBA, or an unsportsmanlike foul under FIBA, is unnecessary or excessive contact, punished with free throws plus possession, and a flagrant two brings an ejection. The escalation matches the intent behind the contact, which is what separates a hard foul from a dangerous one.

In practice

A defender reaches in on a drive and catches the shooter's arm — two free throws. Same defender, same contact, but the shot drops anyway — one free throw for the and-one. Now take the identical reach-in on a player who was not shooting, with the team already over the foul limit: two shots again. And if the contact is a deliberate shove to the head on a fast break, it becomes a flagrant foul, with free throws, possession and possibly an ejection.

Common misunderstandings

  • Every foul sends the fouled player to the free-throw line. Only shooting fouls, fouls by a team over the limit, and technical or flagrant fouls do. Most fouls in a game are punished with a sideline inbound.
  • Players foul out after five fouls everywhere. Six in the NBA, five under FIBA and college rules. The team foul limits differ as well.
  • A technical foul is just a warning. It carries a free throw for the opposition plus possession — one of the most expensive mistakes a player can make in a close game.

Quick questions

What is the bonus?

Once a team passes the foul limit for the period, every defensive foul sends the opponent to the line — even non-shooting fouls. The limit is five in an NBA quarter and four under FIBA and college rules.

What is an and-one?

A shooting foul where the shot still goes in. The basket counts and the player takes one additional free throw — a three- or four-point possession depending on the shot.
