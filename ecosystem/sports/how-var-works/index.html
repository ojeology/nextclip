How VAR actually works — and why it still annoys everyone

BRYME Sport desk · reviewed 2026-09-09 · evergreen explainer — no odds, no tips, no invented facts

In one line: What the video officials check, what they may not touch, why decisions take so long, and the honest trade-off the whole thing bought.

Video Assistant Referee is a support system for the on-field referee, not a parallel authority. That single sentence explains most of what people find baffling about it — the delays, the permissions, and the decisions that end with a shrug toward the pitch.

What VAR is allowed to check

Four categories, and only four: goals (and anything in the build-up that would cancel one), penalty decisions, direct red cards, and mistaken identity (the wrong player booked or sent off). Everything else — yellow cards, fouls outside those moments, the throw-in that went the wrong way — is invisible to VAR by design. The system was built to correct obvious errors in the moments that decide matches, not to re-referee ninety minutes.

The process, step by step

Every checkable incident gets checked silently: the VAR team reviews footage while play continues. Three outcomes. Check complete — nothing to see, play moves on, and you often never learn a check happened. Recommendation to review — the VAR flags a possible clear error; the referee can act on the tip or look themselves at a pitchside screen. On-field review — the referee walks to the monitor, watches the replays, and makes the final call personally. That last point is the architecture: the video officials recommend, the referee decides. When a goal is ruled out after a review and fans say “the computer did that,” it did not — a human on the pitch did, with better views than they had.

Why it takes so long — and the offside exception

The threshold for intervention is “clear and obvious error”, and judgement calls (was that enough contact for a penalty?) are deliberately hard to overturn, which produces long pauses that end in the original decision. Offside is different: it is factual, and at the biggest competitions semi-automated technology generates the lines from tracking data, which is why offside reviews can be quicker than judgement reviews — and why a goal can be cancelled by a toe. For the law those lines are measuring, see the offside rule explained.

The honest trade-off

VAR traded flow for accuracy, and everyone agrees both halves of that sentence. More decisions are correct; every decision now arrives with a pause and a debate about the process. The leagues keep tuning the experience — faster checks, more transparency in stadiums — but the core bargain stands: the moments that decide matches now get a second look, and the second look is here to stay.
