Anime seasons and cours, explained

Anime releases look chaotic from outside — shows appear in January and April and October, run twelve episodes, vanish, and return months later as “Part 2”. The chaos is actually a system, and it has a vocabulary.

The cours

A cour (from French, via Japanese production slang) is a broadcast block of roughly three months — the length of one Japanese TV season. A one-cour series is typically 11 to 13 weekly episodes; a two-cour series runs 24 to 26, either continuously for half a year or split, with the second cour resuming months later. Split cours became common because production is brutal: animating twenty-four episodes to weekly broadcast quality in one stretch is close to impossible, so the pause protects both the staff and the show. When your series ends “at episode 12” and returns as Part 2 next year, that is the system working, not the show being cancelled.

The four seasons

Japanese television turns over four times a year: Winter (January), Spring (April), Summer (July) and Fall (October). Nearly everything premieres at the edge of one of those windows, which is why the anime internet treats New Year's Day, Easter-ish, July and October as “new season” moments. Streaming has blurred the edges — platforms premiere globally now, sometimes hours after Japan — but the seasonal rhythm holds, because broadcast schedules, production timelines and the Japanese academic year all grew up together.

Why adaptations end where they end

Most anime adapts a manga or light novel that is still being written. A cour covers a fixed slice of source story, chosen to land on a workable climax; whether more follows depends on sales, source material and the production committee's appetite. This is why some stories get a decade-later sequel and others stop at one cour — the release pattern is a business decision wearing a creative costume.
