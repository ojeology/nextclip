Why does AFCON move around the calendar?

BRYME Sport desk · reviewed 2026-09-09 · evergreen explainer — no odds, no tips, no invented facts

In one line: The Africa Cup of Nations bounces between January and summer — climate, calendars and television all pull at it. Here is the honest story.

No major tournament changes dates like the Africa Cup of Nations. It has been played in January, February, and mid-year; it has overlapped with European seasons and sat inside summer breaks; and every move sets off the same debate about clubs losing players mid-season. The bouncing is not chaos — it is a real negotiation between forces that genuinely conflict.

The forces pulling the dates

Climate. Some host countries are brutally hot in June and July; a summer edition there means midday kick-offs in dangerous heat, so a cooler-month tournament is better for players and crowds. The European calendar. Most African internationals play for European clubs, and a mid-season AFCON removes them for three-to-four weeks plus a preparation camp — which is why European clubs have lobbied for a summer slot for decades, and why January editions feel like an unpaid tax on African squads. Broadcast value. A tournament against no major competition earns more than one buried beside a European championship or a World Cup summer. And politics and practicality: hosting rights have changed hands for readiness reasons, which pushes dates with them.

Where it has landed lately

The pattern of recent years: tournaments scheduled for the northern-hemisphere winter when the host's climate demands it — Cameroon's edition was played in January–February 2022, Ivory Coast's in January–February 2024 — and the Morocco edition shifted further into December 2025 to January 2026, easing the club-release clash by riding the European winter break. The 2027 edition is set for East Africa, hosted by Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda. Every choice solved one problem and sharpened another; that is the honest summary of AFCON scheduling.

The part worth defending

The January tournament has one underrated virtue: it occupies the African football stage alone. No World Cup to share a summer with, no European championship on the other channel — for a month, the best players on the continent are the main event globally, not a side story. Whether the calendar keeps landing there depends on which force wins the next round: the clubs, the broadcasters, or the climate. If you are new to the tournament, that is also the practical tip: check the dates edition by edition, because unlike almost every other major tournament, AFCON's place in the calendar is a decision, not a tradition.
