English Champions Since 1888: How the Title Was Won, and by Whom

Preston won the first one without losing a game. Twenty-four clubs have won it since. The story of the English title is really the story of four structural changes — and who benefited from each.

Ibrahim Sodiq2026-08-166 min read

It starts with a meeting, not a match

The Football Association codified professional football in 1885. Three years later the Football League was established, after meetings initiated by William McGregor, a director of Aston Villa.

That is the actual origin of the English championship: not a grand founding vision but an administrator solving a scheduling problem for clubs who had just been allowed to pay their players and needed guaranteed fixtures to afford it.

The first season was 1888–89.

Preston won the first one without losing

Preston North End were the first English champions, and they completed their league fixtures unbeaten.

It is worth pausing on how rarely that has been matched in the 130-odd seasons since. Preston did it in the very first attempt, in a competition of twelve clubs, and it remains one of the few genuinely unrepeatable achievements in the sport — not because the football was harder, but because a twelve-team league no longer exists to be won that way.

The league was a northern competition first

For its first four seasons the Football League contained only twelve to fourteen clubs, and every one of them came from the North of England or the Midlands.

This was not a quirk of geography. Professionalism had been embraced more readily in the North and the Midlands than in the South, where the amateur tradition held on longer. The southern clubs that now dominate the English game were, at the point the championship was invented, not part of it at all.

The league expanded in 1892 by absorbing its rival, the Football Alliance. With 28 members it split into two divisions: most former Alliance clubs went into the new Second Division, the original league became the First Division, and promotion and relegation between them began.

That structure — a top division you can be thrown out of — is now so fundamental to English football that it is easy to forget it was an administrative consequence of a merger.

Two changes that moved the money

The two developments that most shaped who wins the title were financial, not sporting.

In 1961 the rules imposing a maximum wage on players were abolished. The effect was immediate and structural: power shifted towards the bigger clubs, because for the first time they could convert greater revenue directly into better players. Before 1961 a wealthy club could not simply outbid a poorer one for a footballer's signature. After it, they could.

In 1992 the clubs then in the First Division defected to form the FA Premier League, which became the new top tier. A series of progressively larger television contracts has since put unprecedented wealth into the hands of top-flight clubs.

Both changes did the same thing in different eras: they widened the gap between what the richest clubs could spend and what everyone else could. Every debate about competitive balance in English football traces back to one or both.

Who has actually won it

Twenty-four clubs have been English champions.

Liverpool and Manchester United share the record with 20 titles each.

The current champions are Arsenal, who won the 2025–26 title — their 14th English championship.

The spread matters as much as the top of the list. Twenty-four different winners across more than a century sounds competitive, and in the early decades it was; the concentration is a recent phenomenon, and it lines up closely with the two financial changes above rather than with anything that happened on a pitch.

A note on counting

Title counts in English football combine three competitions that are really one lineage: the Football League from 1888 to 1892, the Football League First Division from 1892 to 1992, and the Premier League from 1992 onwards.

This is why you will sometimes see two different numbers quoted for the same club — one counting all English championships, the other counting only Premier League titles since 1992. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. Every figure on this page is the full count across all three eras, which is the standard used in the official record.
