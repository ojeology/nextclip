Beginner's Guide to Making Money Online: The Truth Nobody Tells You

An honest beginner guide to building useful digital skills, avoiding online-money traps and earning without chasing shortcuts.

Ibrahim Sodiq2026-08-14

The uncomfortable opening

A phone, internet and free time can help you start learning, but they do not automatically turn into serious money. I spent time chasing gambling, betting, trading, make-money-online schemes and shortcuts because I wanted money quickly. The hard lesson was that the boring work people avoid — coding, writing, design and real practice — is usually where the long-term value is.

1. There is no holy grail online

Legitimate online work asks for skill, time, knowledge, creativity, problem-solving or consistent effort. Ask who is paying, what work is being done, why the payment makes sense, whether the model is transparent, and whether claims can be checked. “Make ₦500,000 in seven days with no skills” should make you suspicious, not excited. If you must pay before you can supposedly earn, treat that as a major warning sign.

2. Stop chasing quick money — build a digital skill

Coding, writing and graphic design are strong examples, not the only options. Video editing, digital marketing, data analysis, UI/UX, virtual assistance, cybersecurity and specialised AI-related work can also be useful. Earning potential tends to grow when your ability, experience, reputation and usefulness grow.

3. Test skills instead of waiting for the perfect one

Try coding, writing, design or another skill that interests you. Give each one focused time, then ask: does it keep my attention, do I want to return to it, can I practise it consistently, and am I willing to become good at it? ChatGPT can help you explore options based on your interests, but it cannot magically choose a career for you.

4. You can start with a phone

I started learning coding on a phone. A phone can be enough to begin many digital skills, although coding is slower and some tools or libraries are difficult to use without a computer. Do not wait for an expensive setup before you start; improve your equipment as your ability and income grow.

5. Learn without drowning in tutorials

Do not collect hundreds of random tutorials. Start with fundamentals, one concept at a time, practise regularly and ask questions when stuck. You can tell an AI tutor: “I want to learn coding. I am a complete beginner. Teach me step by step and make it easy and enjoyable.” Use AI to explain ideas, not to blindly copy answers. Documentation, books and courses remain useful as you progress.

6. Competence first. Income follows.

Do not spend your first months asking exactly when money will arrive. That pressure can make you rush past fundamentals. Learn, practise and become comfortable with the work first. There is no honest universal timeline for income.

7. When to seek paid work

You do not need to be an expert before taking a small job, but you must be able to deliver it properly. Appropriate routes can include freelance and remote-work platforms, writing opportunities, AI/data-work platforms, direct clients, local businesses and professional networks. Platform rules, assessments and availability change; none guarantees work.

8. Make your ability visible

Build a clear LinkedIn profile, CV, portfolio, demonstration projects and professional communication. Clients need evidence. A small project that shows what you can do is more persuasive than simply saying “I know how to code.”

9. Writing can be an entry point

If you communicate well, writing can be a practical starting skill. Clients may give a topic, length, style, deadline and payment terms; your job is to deliver useful work. AI can assist research, brainstorming, editing and structure, but it should not replace genuine thinking or produce junk. Rejected work may become portfolio material only when client rights and confidentiality allow it.

10. Do not take work you cannot do

Do not pretend you can deliver a high-paying job in a language or skill you do not know. Poor work damages trust, overwhelms you and can pull you away from the skill you were actually building. Choose work you can realistically complete well.

11. The first money may be tiny

Your first legitimate income may be cents, a few dollars or a small freelance payment. That is not failure. The point is to separate a small temporary task from building a capability that can lead to larger opportunities.

12. Treat microtasks with extreme caution

Microtasks can sometimes pay small amounts, but they are not a path to wealth. Never pay money to access a supposed microtask opportunity. Check rates, withdrawal requirements, country availability, minimum payout, terms and reputation. Some tasks pay very little for the time they take.

13. The biggest scam warning

“Make $100 every day with no experience”, “turn ₦20,000 into ₦500,000 in one week”, “guaranteed income”, “no skills required” and “pay to unlock your account” are reasons to stop and investigate. Understand the business model before giving anyone money.

14. Do not quit your job too early

A safer sequence is: normal job, learn a skill, small online income, more experience, more clients, consistent income, then financial stability. Do not abandon an existing income source because you have only started learning. Multiple income sources can be safer than one risky leap.

15. Upgrade equipment with a reason

Start with what you have. As income grows, improve your phone, laptop, internet or tools when they genuinely make you more productive. Do not go into debt for expensive equipment before you need it.

16. What consistency can make possible

Two or three years of serious practice can open possibilities: remote work, freelance clients, websites, software, digital products, a business or multiple income sources. My own coding journey eventually helped me build a website, a trading bot and other projects. That is personal experience, not a promise of the same result for everyone.

17. A skill can become more than a job

A valuable skill can grow from freelance work to clients, reputation, business, products and multiple income streams. That path takes time, proof of work and continued learning.

18. The hard part is consistency

Learning gets boring, frustrating, difficult and confusing. I felt that while learning coding. Treat each error as a puzzle to solve, one step at a time. Hard work does not guarantee wealth, but quitting every difficult thing guarantees you never get the chance to improve.

The BRYME conclusion

There is nothing wrong with wanting money. The mistake is believing money should arrive without value being created. If making money online was that easy, nobody would be doing the hard work. A phone and internet can open doors, but a valuable skill helps you walk through them. Stop chasing the shortcut. Build something valuable.
