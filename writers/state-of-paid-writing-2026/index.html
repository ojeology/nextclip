The State of Paid Writing, 2026 edition.

By BRYME Editorial Desk · Updated 2026-08-19 to 2026-10-01

What 288 real, currently-listed literary and journalism markets actually pay, what they accept, and what they do not tell you. Every figure on this page is computed from the BRYME opportunities database at build time — 288 publication records, each human-verified against its official guidelines page between 2026-08-19 to 2026-10-01. No figure is estimated, and "not stated" is reported as a finding.

288 publication recordsVerified 2026-08-19 to 2026-10-01Browse the records

The window right now

221 of 288 markets are open or rolling as of this build; 4 carry a fixed deadline, 30 open later, 25 are closed, and 8 could not be confirmed. Statuses flip record by record in today's list, which recomputes on every deploy.

What markets pay, where they say

Rates below are the stated minimums on official guidelines pages — the floor of what an accepted piece earns, in the currency the market itself quotes. We do not convert currencies and we do not average across them.

  • US-dollar markets (129): stated minimums run $3 to $2,500, with a median of $30 per accepted piece.
  • Nigerian-naira markets (5): stated minimums run ₦5,000 to ₦150,000, median ₦50,000.
  • 154 records quote other currencies, pay per word, or do not publish a number at all.

The honest caveat: a stated rate is what the guidelines promise on the day we checked. The window above is the verification range — reopen the official page before you pitch.

The AI question, answered by the markets themselves

140 of 288 publications state an AI policy on their guidelines page, and 127 of those 140 prohibit AI-generated submissions outright; 8 require disclosure and 5 allow limited use. The largest single group — 148 — says nothing yet, which is itself the finding: most literary markets have not published an AI policy, and silence is not permission.

What the guidelines do not tell you

  • Rights: 80 of 288 records state clearly that copyright stays with the author — as "copyright remains with the author", "all rights revert to the author on publication" or "authors retain". A further 69 address rights but say only what the publication acquires, never who ends up owning the work. The remaining 139 do not address rights at all. Ask before you sign anything.
  • Response times: 139 markets do not publicly state a response window. Where they do, it lives on each record.

What they are looking for

Across the 288 records, the most-requested forms are: fiction (180), poetry (156), essays (108), creative nonfiction (105), reviews (44), journalism (31). Submissions run through 88 Submittable portals; the rest split between email pitches, online forms and other routes — each record shows its own method.

How to read this report

It describes the 288 publications BRYME tracks, not the whole market. It counts what official guidelines state, not what contributors anecdotally report. Where a market states a rate for one format only, that is what we counted. The full method — and every record behind every number here — is open: the database, opportunities by country, and the editorial policy that governs all of it.

Desk notes: how to read a year-survey honestly

This survey aggregates what the desk could verify — rates published in guidelines, recorded with read-dates — and its honest limitation is stated on the page: it is a snapshot of observable listings, not a census of the industry. Pair it with two habits to make it useful: read the methodology note before the numbers, and treat every figure as a floor-finder for the rate research you still have to do per market.

The desk's standing context for any pay survey: rates disperse around genres and rights as much as around skill — the contract guide explains the rights half of that variance — and the realised number that matters is yours, tracked in the tracker. Industry-wide context lives with the trade bodies and databases the desk cites throughout, including Poets & Writers' tools and reports.

What the year changed

The paid writing market in 2026 runs on the same two engines as ever — expertise and access — with AI slop pushing editors toward bylines with receipts. Rates hold at the top and compress in the middle; the writers who report, invoice and niche survive the compression. The literary press this desk follows tracks the shifts. By the Bryme Editorial desk. Reviewed 28 September 2026.

The findings that matter

  • Reported work holds value. Verification is the moat now.
  • Niches price better. The specialist's rate is the survivor's rate.
  • The middle is squeezed. General explainers compete with the machine.
