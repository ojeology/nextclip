Desk notes: process is personal, parts are not

Every writer's process is idiosyncratic and every good process contains the same four stages: capture (ideas in, before they evaporate), draft (fast, judgement-free), revise (structure before sentences), and ship (the deadline that converts writing into work). This section's guides walk all four — and the desk's advice is to steal the stages while ignoring everyone's rituals, including ours. The mysticism around process ("I need the exact pen") is procrastination with props.

The external backbone: the Purdue OWL's writing-process resources — reachable from the OWL index — lay out the standard stages with exercises, and they pair cleanly with this section's habit guides. What the formal resources omit, the desk's own pages supply: the paid-world half of the process (pitching, invoicing, the tracker) that turns finished drafts into a career.

The calibration most writers need: find which stage you secretly skip. Non-starters usually skip capture (nothing to draft from); chronic blockers skip drafting discipline (judging while generating); perpetual-polishers skip shipping (revising as hiding). The section's guides are ordered so you can diagnose your skipped stage and fix exactly that — and the starting guides are the floor under all of it.

The four stages, scheduled into a real week

The process section's stages become a career when they get calendar slots, so here is the desk's honest scheduling for a working writer with more obligations than hours: capture runs continuously — one inbox for ideas, reviewed Friday (five minutes); draft gets the morning's first hour or nothing, three days a week, timer-ruled per the starting script; revise gets its own afternoon on pieces that have rested overnight — never the same session as drafting, per the section's core rule; ship gets Friday — submissions, invoices, the tracker sweep, the follow-ups from the timing table.

The schedule's quiet insight: shipping day is what makes the other three stages a career rather than a hobby, and it is the stage writers skip when the others run long. Guard it. The Purdue OWL's writing-process pages carry the formal stage descriptions; the calendar above is what one desk actually runs.

The loop that repeats

A writing process is a loop, not a ladder: draft fast with the editor off, rest the draft, edit with the reader on, then proof with the language tools out. Writers who skip the rest stage edit too early and sand the life out of the work. The writing process is documented in standard references. By the Bryme Writers desk. Reviewed 28 September 2026.

The four stations

  • Draft. Momentum beats quality here; nothing said in draft is final.
  • Rest. Time is the cheapest editing tool available.
  • Edit. Structure, argument, cuts — the reader's needs in order.
  • Proof. Spelling and rhythm last, never first.
