Desk notes: problems are patterns, and patterns have fixes

Almost every stuck draft traces to one of a small set of patterns: no thesis (the piece has energy but no destination), buried lede (the real first sentence hides in paragraph four), scope creep (three essays wearing one title), or the fear spiral that mistakes drafting for judgement. This section exists so you can diagnose by pattern instead of by despair — naming the problem is half the fix, which is why the guides here are short and specific.

The desk's standing advice for the meta-problem ("my writing is bad"): bad is a draft's permanent address, not its fate. The editing section carries the repair routines, the process section prevents the most common causes, and the Purdue OWL's general writing resources hold the formal versions of nearly every fix this section teaches.

One honest note about block: it is usually decision fatigue wearing a dramatic name. When the page stalls, the question is rarely "why can't I write" and usually "which of two next moves haven't I chosen?" Pick either one; drafts survive wrong turns better than stalled ones, and the starting guides cover the lowest-friction ways back in.

The diagnosis table, expanded

Because patterns deserve names you can search for, here is the section's expanded diagnostic: the piece has no ending — usually a missing thesis back at the top (the ending is the thesis, kept); every sentence is the same length — read the piece aloud and break the metronome at the load-bearing moments; the research is showing — you wrote the process, not the finding; move the journey to a footnote and the conclusion to paragraph one; you have rewritten paragraph one nine times — that is not editing, that is avoidance with a red pen; write paragraph two badly and keep moving. Each diagnosis links to a repair: the editing section for the passes, the starting guides for the avoidance, and the Purdue OWL's revision resources for the formal versions of all of them.

The meta-rule the section keeps proving: problems that survive three passes are structure problems wearing sentence costumes. Go up one level of outline and look again.

The section's own escalation path, for the problem that outlives its diagnosis: if a draft has resisted two structured passes and still reads wrong, the honest next step is not a third pass but a verdict — the piece may be two pieces, or the wrong piece, or a piece whose time has passed. Killing a draft is a professional skill, and the desk's rejection guide applies to your own manuscripts exactly as to pitches: salvage the working part (there is always a working paragraph), log the lesson in the process notes, and start the next one lighter. The examples shelf is the antidote of last resort — one hour with good published prose reminds the hands what the target feels like.

The problems cluster

Writers bring the same handful of complaints: the introduction will not land, the prose sounds like a committee, and finished drafts read long. Each is a symptom of a fixable habit rather than a talent gap — usually structure decided too late, or editing skipped as a stage. The writing craft is documented in standard references. By the Bryme Writers desk. Reviewed 28 September 2026.

Where each problem starts

  • Weak openings usually mean the writer is still deciding the point. Decide first.
  • Formal distance usually means writing to impress rather than to inform.
  • Endless drafts usually mean no finishing rule. Set the cut line before you edit.
