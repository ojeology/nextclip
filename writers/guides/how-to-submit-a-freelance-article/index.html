Writing guide

How to submit a freelance article

From reading the guidelines to sending the finished piece the right way.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

In this guide
  1. Read the submission instructions
  2. Formatting and files
  3. Attachments and clips
  4. The submission channel
  5. Track and confirm

Submitting a freelance article is where many writers stumble over boring details, and details matter.

Read the submission instructions to the end. The guideline tells you whether they want a pitch or the full article, the word count, the format and the channel.

Format it the way they asked. Some want a docx; some want text in an email; some use a form. Do not guess when the answer is in the guideline.

Attach exactly what is required. A cover note or clips if asked. Do not add a long cover letter if they did not ask for one.

Use the official channel. An email, a form, or a submission portal from the publication — never an invented address.

Track and confirm. Note the date you sent it and, if you can, confirm receipt. If there is no stated response time, it is reasonable to follow up after a couple of weeks.

Desk notes: submission mechanics, done once, done right

Most submissions fail on mechanics, not merit, so the desk keeps this page boring on purpose. The pre-flight: guidelines read on the publication's own site (the opportunity desk links each one directly, with the date we last verified it), the pitch or article in the requested form, the requested subject line, and the bio line written to fit. Editors do not reject for missing fluff; they do bounce for ignored instructions.

The email itself: subject line per guidelines or "PITCH: [working title]" if none given; the pitch in the body of the email unless attachments are invited; links not attachments for samples — the samples guide covers what to link. Send weekday mornings in the publication's time zone; log it immediately in the tracker with the date, so the waiting arithmetic starts from fact.

And the house rule on exclusivity: unless the guidelines say simultaneous submissions are unwelcome, say so honestly in your pitch ("this is a simultaneous submission") if it is one. The publications that forbid simultaneity are telling you their response speed in advance — factor that into the follow-up plan.

The pre-send pass, as a list

The guide above describes the mechanics; here they are as the literal sixty-second pass the desk runs before anything goes out: guidelines read on the publication's own page this week (not from memory, not from a repost — the opportunity desk links them with read-dates); the requested format honoured (body vs attachment, doc type, length); the subject line per guidelines or "PITCH: [title]"; the bio line present and one sentence; samples linked, not attached, unless invited; the timeline honest ("available from [date]"); and the tracker updated the moment the send happens, so the follow-up clock starts from fact.

The style layer on top of the mechanics: the email itself is a writing sample — the pitch that demonstrates the requested register in five clean sentences is the strongest cover letter possible, and the Purdue OWL's professional-correspondence sections are the reference for the conventions if they are new to you.

The Pre-Send Pass

Submitting a freelance article is a mechanical process done once and done right: read the guidelines, format to the house style, write the cover note the market expects, and run the pre-send pass as a checklist.

  • Read the guidelines Every one, every time; they change without notice.
  • Format to house Manuscript format versus the outlet's own template.
  • The cover note Three sentences: piece, why here, who you are.
  • The pre-send pass The checklist that prevents every common return.
  • Simultaneous submissions Policy honesty before sending elsewhere.
  • The rights question Know what you are selling before you sell it.

Submission mechanics are learnable once and reused forever. The pre-send pass turns anxious sending into ordinary process.
