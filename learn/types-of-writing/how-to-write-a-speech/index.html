Types of writing

How to write a speech

Write spoken words that sound natural, land on one message, and hold an audience.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Types of writing guides

A speech is written to be heard, not read. The rules differ from written prose: sentences are shorter, the rhythm matters more, and everything points to one central message the audience remembers.

What it is

A speech is spoken text meant to inform, persuade, or move an audience in a live (or recorded) setting. It is written for the ear, and it is meant to be delivered with a voice.

Why it matters

Spoken language is more forgiving but harder to follow than written text. A speech that reads well on paper can sound flat or confusing aloud if it is not shaped for delivery.

How to write it

Know your one message. A speech should make one clear point. Ask: what is the single thing I want the audience to remember? Everything else supports it.

Know your audience. What do they already know, what do they care about, and what do you want them to think or do? Write for them, not for yourself.

Start strong. Open with a line that earns attention — a question, a story, a startling fact, or a direct statement. Do not begin with "thank you for having me" or a lengthy introduction.

Keep sentences short and spoken. Short sentences are easier to hear and remember. Favor subject-verb-object. Read each line aloud and reword anything you stumble on.

Use rhythm and repetition. Repetition of a phrase builds emphasis and helps an audience follow ("We are not going to wait. We are not going to give up."). Vary the length for effect.

Tell a story or give a concrete example. A concrete example makes an abstract point land. People remember stories and images more than arguments.

Build a clear structure. Opening → the point → supporting beats → a strong close. Signpost transitions so the audience knows where they are.

End with something that lands. Do not fade out. Restate the point, call the audience to action, or close on a memorable line. The last thing they hear is what they remember.

Typical structure

  1. Opening — a hook that earns attention.
  2. The message — stated clearly.
  3. Supporting beats — stories, evidence, examples, in a build.
  4. Close — the point restated, a call to action, or a land.

Common mistakes

Writing it like an essay to be read silently. Too many points. Starting with a long thank-you or throat-clearing. Sentences too long to deliver. No clear message. Fading out at the end.

Practical tips

  • Write for the ear — read every line aloud.
  • Cut anything that is not part of the one message.
  • Keep it short; a tight speech beats a long rambling one.
  • Mark your delivery cues (pause, slow, emphasise) in the margin, not in the body.
