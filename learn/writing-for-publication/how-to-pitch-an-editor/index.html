Writing for publication

How to pitch an editor

Approach editors well, pitch the right idea, and follow up without burning bridges.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Writing for publication guides

Pitching is a professional conversation, not a request for a favour. Editors want good ideas that fit their publication and that they can trust a writer to deliver. Pitch that way and you stand out.

What it is

A pitch is your proposal of a specific piece to a specific publication. The editor, who has limited time and a clear view of what they want, decides quickly whether the idea, the fit, and the writer are right.

Why it matters

The pitch, not the writing, is often what gets you in the door. A well-targeted pitch shows you understand the publication and can deliver what they need — which is worth more than a pile of unasked manuscripts.

How to pitch well

Match the idea to the publication. Pick a piece that genuinely fits — its topic, length, tone and audience. A great idea for the wrong market is still a no.

Read the guidelines first. Follow the exact submission method, word count, and rules. Most rejections are simply guideline violations.

Pitch one specific piece. Not "I can write about business," but one angle, one argument, one story. Specific ideas get answered; vague offers do not.

Show you have done your homework. Mention a relevant recent piece they ran. Reference the reporting or material you already have. This signals you will not waste their time.

Be easy to say yes to. State the length, deadline, and what you need. Make it easy to imagine the finished piece.

Address the right person. Use the name of the section editor if you can find it, and the email or form they provide. A generic "Dear Editor" is weaker.

Follow up once, politely. After a reasonable wait, one short note is fine. Treat no reply after a follow-up as a likely pass.

When to send a full manuscript

Some pages — especially literary magazines and personal-essay publications — ask for full pieces, not queries. Always follow what the guideline asks for.

Common mistakes

Sending a generic proposal to many publications. Pitching a topic instead of a story. Not reading the guidelines. Arguing with a rejection. Pitching an idea already covered by a recent issue. Asking editors to "check my portfolio" instead of offering a piece.

Practical tips

  • Keep the pitch short — an editor should understand it in two lines.
  • Mention one thing that shows you read them.
  • Keep a record of every pitch: the market, the date, and the outcome.
