Writing for publication

How to handle a rejection

Treat rejection as data — respond well, learn from it, and keep pitching without losing momentum.

Intermediate Assumes you can already draft and revise a piece.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Writing for publication guides

Rejection is part of every writer's career — it is not a verdict on you as a writer, but a signal about one idea, one market, one moment. The writers who succeed are not the ones who escape rejection; they are the ones who keep going.

What it is

Rejection is an editor declining your pitch or manuscript. It happens for many reasons: the idea does not fit, the publication already covered it, the angle is wrong, the piece is too long, or they simply have a full slate.

Why it matters

Your response to rejection shapes your career. Treat it as data and you keep improving and keep pitching. Treat it as a personal failure and you stop writing.

How to respond well

Take a breath and a short break. Do not reply immediately. Read the rejection once and put it down. Distance helps.

Thank the editor. A one-line gracious reply is professional and leaves the door open. "Thank you for your time, and for considering it." Never argue or ask them to reconsider at length.

Look for the useful signal. If they said why (too long, wrong market, covered it), that is valuable. If they did not, infer what you can and move on.

Decide the idea's next move. Is the piece right for a different market? Does it need restructuring? Or was it simply a fit problem with nothing to fix? Not every rejection needs a change.

Keep a record. Note the market, date, and any feedback. Patterns emerge: your pitches may be too vague, your length off, or your market choice wrong.

Keep pitching. Send the idea to a suitable next market (if you have told the first editor you would, do so), or pitch a fresh idea. Momentum beats perfection.

Do not take it personally. Editors reject ideas and fit, not people. One market's pass is rarely a judgment on your whole body of work.

Common mistakes

Replying with an argument. Rewriting the whole piece for a single vague rejection. Giving up pitching because of one no. Sending the same rejected pitch to the same editor again. Letting a rejection stop you from writing.

Practical tips

  • Remember even famous writers collect rejections.
  • Pitch two or more ideas at a time so no single no can stop you.
  • Reuse what you learn: your next pitch should be better than the last.
