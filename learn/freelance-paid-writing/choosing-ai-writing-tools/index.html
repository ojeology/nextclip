Using AI tools as a writer without losing markets

41 of 142 verified publications prohibit AI-assisted work, one of them contractually and including grammar tools. Read the policy before you choose the tool.

Intermediate Assumes you can already draft and revise a piece.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Freelance & paid writing guides

Most guides to AI writing tools compare features. That is the wrong first question for a working writer, because the binding constraint is not what a tool can do — it is what your market allows.

BRYME has not run these tools as a paying customer and does not rank them. What follows is the policy landscape you have to write inside, and how to use AI without losing the markets you want.

Start with the policy, not the tool

Of the 142 publications BRYME has verified against their own guidelines:

PolicyPublications
Prohibited41
Disclosure required4
Limited / restricted3
Not stated89

Nearly a third of the market says no outright. And the wording is often broader than writers assume:

  • The Fiction Desk requires you to confirm no AI use on the submission form and again in the publishing contract — and its definition explicitly includes grammar and punctuation correction tools.
  • Island states: "Please don't send us anything you've written, rewritten or otherwise created using AI. AI is destroying writers' income."
  • Prairie Fire refuses work using "AI, algorithmic, machine-learning, or computer-generated text of any sort."
  • Augur treats undisclosed AI use as a breach of contract even after acceptance.
  • EVENT names ChatGPT specifically.
  • The Tyee states it does not publish journalism written or generated by AI, with its own linked policy.
  • Orion repeats the prohibition three separate times on one page.

The most thoughtful policy found is The /tEmz/ Review, which permits AI where there is a legitimate artistic reason, provided you state the extent and the reasons in your cover letter, and it is disclosed to readers. That is the only one drawing the distinction between using a tool and passing off a machine's work as your own.

What this means practically

"Not stated" is not permission. Eighty-nine publications say nothing. BRYME does not read silence as consent and neither should you. If it matters, ask.

The definition varies enormously. One publication means "don't have a model write it." Another means "don't run a grammar checker over it." Those are wildly different obligations and the phrase "no AI" covers both.

Contractual bans survive acceptance. With The Fiction Desk and Augur, you are signing something. Detection is unreliable and both have solved that by moving the risk onto you contractually, which does not require detection to work.

Where AI is genuinely useful and low-risk

None of the below involves a machine producing text you publish under your name:

Research organisation. Summarising sources you have already gathered and verified, building comparison tables from your own notes, finding the through-line in an interview transcript.

Structural feedback. "What is the weakest section here and why" is a useful question. "Rewrite this" is a different act.

Pitch preparation. Checking your pitch answers the questions an editor will ask.

Admin. Chasing emails, scheduling, formatting a bibliography.

Verification support. Generating a list of claims in your draft that need checking. You still check them.

Where it costs you

Generating prose you submit. Prohibited by 41 of 142 markets, and the ones paying best are disproportionately among them.

Fabricated citations. The fastest way to end a professional relationship, especially in [medical or technical writing](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/medical-writing-rates/) where accuracy is the product.

Flattening your voice. The commercial value of a writer is the thing a model cannot supply. Sanding that off to save an hour is a bad trade — and it is why, on the survey data, rates at the professional end rose in the year AI adoption spread while generic content collapsed in price.

A workable policy for yourself

  1. Check the market's policy before you start, not before you submit.
  2. Never let a tool generate text you will publish as your own.
  3. Keep correction and rewriting separate, and know which your tools do.
  4. Disclose where disclosure is invited — The /tEmz/ Review's approach is where the market is heading.
  5. Verify every factual claim yourself, regardless of source.
  6. Assume policies will tighten. They have moved a long way in eighteen months.

Choosing a tool, if you use one

Since the policy comes first, the tool questions are narrow:

  • Can I turn generation off and keep only research and organisation?
  • What happens to my text? Retention matters under embargo or NDA.
  • Does it cite sources I can check, or produce claims I cannot trace?
  • Does it silently rewrite anything I did not ask it to?
