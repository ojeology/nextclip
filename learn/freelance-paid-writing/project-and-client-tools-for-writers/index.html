Project and client management tools for writers — what you actually need

A solo writer with six clients does not have a project management problem. Here is how to tell whether you need a tool at all, and what to check if you do.

Beginner Start here — assumes no prior knowledge.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Freelance & paid writing guides

BRYME has not run these tools as a paying customer over months, so this does not rank them. It is a way of working out whether you need one, because most freelance writers do not, and the category is built to convince you otherwise.

A note on scope: project management and client management are sold as two separate products, and for a solo writer they are one problem — knowing what you owe, to whom, by when, and whether you have been paid. Splitting this into two guides would produce two near-identical articles, so it is one.

The test

You need a tool when you can answer yes to at least two of these:

  • I have lost track of a deadline in the last three months
  • I have forgotten to invoice for completed work
  • I regularly manage more than five or six live commissions at once
  • I subcontract to other writers, or work with an editor who needs visibility
  • I cannot answer "what am I owed right now?" in under a minute

If you answered no to all of them, you have a list problem, not a software problem. A single document with four columns solves it and costs nothing.

The four things a writer actually tracks

Strip away the feature lists and this is the job:

WhatWhy it matters
Pitches sentSo you know what to follow up and when to stop
Commissions liveDeadline, agreed fee, word count, editor
Delivered, awaiting paymentThe one writers lose money on
PaidSo you can see the year honestly

That is four states. Any tool that cannot show you those four clearly is solving somebody else's problem.

BRYME's [submission tracker](/tracker/) does exactly this, runs in your browser, stores nothing on a server, and costs nothing. For most writers it is enough, and it was built because the general-purpose tools are overkill.

If you do need something bigger

Check these, in order:

1. Does it model "paid on publication"? Editorial work is frequently paid months after delivery. A tool that marks everything overdue on net-30 will nag your editors on your behalf — see [how to invoice a publication](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/how-to-invoice-as-a-writer/). This single mismatch makes most business tools actively harmful for writers.

2. Can you get your data out? A client list you cannot export is a hostage. Check CSV export before you put two years of work in.

3. Does it work at the size you actually are? Tools priced per seat and built for teams add ceremony a solo writer pays for in time. Kanban boards, dependencies and sprints solve coordination problems you do not have.

4. Does it duplicate what you already pay for? Many invoicing tools already track clients and payment status. Adding a CRM on top means maintaining the same information twice, which is how both become wrong.

5. Does it cost less than the time it saves? At a realistic hourly rate — check yours with the [rate calculator](/tools/freelance-rate-calculator/) — a £20/month tool needs to save roughly fifteen to twenty minutes a month to break even. That is a low bar, but only if you actually use it.

The failure mode

Tool churn. Setting up a new system feels productive and is not. Writers who move from Trello to Notion to Asana to ClickHouse in a year have spent that year on configuration, not commissions.

Pick one, badly, and stay. A mediocre system you actually update beats an elegant one you abandon in March.

Free options that are genuinely sufficient

  • BRYME's [submission tracker](/tracker/) — pitches, deadlines, payment status, in the browser
  • A spreadsheet — four columns, sorted by deadline. Unglamorous and undefeated
  • Notion or Trello free tiers — if you want something more structured
  • Your invoicing software — most already track client and payment status, so check before adding anything
