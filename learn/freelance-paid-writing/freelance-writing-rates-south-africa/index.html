Freelance writing rates in South Africa — the SAFREA rate card vs reality

South Africa is one of the few markets with a published freelance rate card. What SAFREA's figures say, what publications and clients actually pay, and how to price between the two.

Intermediate Assumes you can already draft and revise a piece.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Freelance & paid writing guides

South Africa is unusual on this continent: it has professional bodies that publish actual recommended rates — the SAFREA rate card being the most widely cited. That makes the gap between what writers should charge and what clients do pay unusually easy to see.

It is a real gap. Both sets of numbers belong in your next quote.

What the SAFREA card says

The SAFREA (South African Freelancers' Association) rate ranges, as widely republished across the SA media industry press:

WorkPer wordPer hour
Magazine writingR3–R4R400–R500
Newspaper writingR2–R3R400–R500
Corporate contentR2–R4R400–R600
Technical / specialisedR4+R400–R700
GhostwritingR5–R7R450–R550
Website / SEO contentR1.50–R3.50R450–R550
Press release (flat)—R2,000+

The structure is the useful part, even more than the figures: hourly floors are remarkably consistent (R400–R600 across almost everything), and per-word rates scale with consequence — ghostwriting and technical content sit two to three times above general web copy. Note what the card implies: nobody's floor is R1 a word, and "SEO content" is not priced as a commodity by the people who wrote this card.

What the market actually pays

Independent 2026 pricing guides put working South African writers at entry levels of roughly R150–R200 an hour, mid-level copywriters R250–R400, and specialists (SEO, financial, technical, medical) R400–R600+ — with blog work commonly landing R2.50–R3.50 a word and technical or financial writing R4–R8. Payscale's self-reported median (about R150/hour) sits far below the card, which is what self-selected survey data does; treat it as the floor of the floor, not the market.

The pattern matches what this site sees across its verified [opportunity record](/writing-opportunities/south-africa/): local publications pay real money by regional standards, corporate and agency work pays better than editorial, and the clients who respect the rate card are the ones who already answer emails fast.

The gap, stated plainly

The card describes the top of the market and the corporate buyers who know what writing costs. Bottom-of-market platforms and one-off clients will offer a quarter of it. Neither number is a lie; they are different buyers. The professional move is the same one this site teaches everywhere: price from the [rate calculator](/tools/freelance-rate-calculator/) and the buyer's market, not from your costs.

And South African writers have the same lever this site documents for the whole continent: a [US client pays against the US rate card](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/freelance-writing-rates-us/), not against Johannesburg costs. The [international positioning guide](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/african-writers-international-markets/) covers the exchange-rate side — a card rate of R500 an hour is very good locally and underpriced in dollars.

What this should change

  1. Quote corporate and agency work against the card's hourly floors — R400+ is the published norm, not a fantasy.
  2. Never quote technical or specialist work at web-copy rates; the card's own structure says otherwise.
  3. Treat R1/word offers as what they are: the bottom of a documented market, not "the market".
  4. For international clients, price in the client's currency against the [relevant rate guide](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/freelance-writing-rates-uk/) — and see [cross-border contracts](/learn/freelance-paid-writing/cross-border-writing-business/) before signing anything with a foreign governing-law clause.

The two markets in one line

South Africa has one writing market that reads the rate card and one that has never heard of it — the entire game is choosing which room you are pricing in.

SAFREA ranges reflect the rate card as republished across South African media-industry press; independent 2026 pricing guides were cross-checked at publication time (7 September 2026). Rates vary by client, sector and experience — this is market information, not advice.
