Freelance & paid writing

The highest-paying writing niches, with real survey data

What medical, technical, financial and SaaS writing actually pay, according to the EFA, EMWA and ProCopywriters rate surveys.

Intermediate Assumes you can already draft and revise a piece.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Freelance & paid writing guides

Most advice about "high-paying writing niches" is written by people selling a course. This guide uses published rate surveys instead, names each one, and tells you the sample size so you can judge the number yourself.

Why niche beats skill for income

Two writers with identical ability can earn three times differently, and the variable is almost never talent. It is subject matter. The pattern across every survey below is the same: rates rise where the writing carries regulatory risk, requires domain knowledge the client cannot fake, or directly moves money.

Put plainly — a client pays more when getting it wrong is expensive for them.

What the surveys actually say

Medical and scientific writing: the top of the table

The Editorial Freelancers Association 2026 Rate Chart is based on a survey of over 1,100 members — more than a third of the EFA's membership — run from November 2025 to mid-January 2026, covering the 2025 calendar year.

Writing categoryPer wordPer hour
Medical$1.00–$1.50$90–$126
Scientific$1.00–$1.20$87.50–$113
Technical87.5¢–$1.08$75–$126
Journalism40¢–50¢$75–$125
Business/marketing32¢–87.5¢$65–$75

Blog posts25¢–45¢$74.50–$87.50

Read the top and bottom rows together. Medical writing pays roughly four times what blog writing pays per word in the same survey, from the same pool of freelancers.

The EFA is careful to say it neither sets rates nor advises members on what to charge, and that individual rates vary with turnaround, expertise, cost of living, training and experience. Treat the chart as a benchmark, not a price list.

The European picture

The EMWA Freelance Business Survey, published in the European Medical Writers Association's journal, found an overall median hourly rate of €82.20 across all services, broken down by service type:

  • Consultancy — €109
  • Training — €99.90
  • Regulatory writing — €95
  • Non-regulatory writing — €80
  • Translation — €65

And by country: France €92.50, Germany €82.50, United Kingdom €79.00, Italy €63.80, Portugal €57.90, India €23.00.

That last spread matters if you are pricing from outside Europe or North America. The rate follows the client's market, not your postcode — but the survey shows the gap is real and worth negotiating against.

UK copywriting

The ProCopywriters annual survey is the clearest UK benchmark. Its 2025 edition, based on 474 copywriters, put the average freelance day rate at £480, up £40 on the previous year. The 2026 survey reports a median of £440 — the gap between the two figures is the usual mean-versus-median effect, where a small number of very high rates pull the average up.

The detail that matters more than the headline: rates rose in a year when almost everyone expected AI to push them down. The survey's own reading is that the mechanical part of writing got cheaper while strategy, positioning and accountability did not.

Broken down by experience and specialism, as reported in market analyses drawing on that survey:

  • Junior (0–2 years): £250–£350/day
  • Mid-level (3–7 years): £400–£600/day
  • Senior (8+ years): £500–£800/day
  • Financial services and fintech: £600–£850/day
  • Technology and software: £450–£750/day
  • Charity and non-profit: £250–£375/day

Note the bottom entry. Non-profit work is the lowest-paid copywriting there is, and it is where a lot of new writers start because the door is open. That is a defensible choice — just make it knowingly.

The pattern worth internalising

Rank those numbers and one rule emerges:

You get paid for the consequences of being wrong.

Medical and regulatory writing tops every table because an error can harm a patient or fail an audit. Financial and insurance copy is next because an error can breach a regulator's rules. Technical documentation follows because an error costs engineering hours. Blog content sits at the bottom because nothing much happens if a paragraph is mediocre.

This is good news, because "consequences" is learnable in a way that "talent" is not.

How to choose a niche without a science degree

You do not need a PhD to enter most of these. What you need is proof you can be trusted with the subject.

Start from what you already know. Everyone has an industry they understand from a previous job, a family business, or a long-standing interest. That is domain knowledge, and it is the scarce half of the equation.

Pick a niche with a paper trail. Regulated industries publish endlessly — guidance, standards, filings. You can learn the vocabulary in public, for free, without a gatekeeper.

Write one credible sample. Not ten. One piece that demonstrates you understand the subject and its conventions will do more than a portfolio of general blog posts.

Price from the survey, not from your nerves. Use the [freelance rate calculator](/tools/freelance-rate-calculator/) to work out what you need, then check it against the benchmarks above. If your number is far below them, the problem is your pricing, not the market.
