How to write a conclusion

End a piece so it lands — restate the point, give it meaning, and avoid new arguments.

Beginner Start here — assumes no prior knowledge.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Structure & formatting guides

The conclusion is where a piece finishes, but it is not where the argument happens. Its job is to land what you have built and give the reader a reason to have read it. A good conclusion feels like an ending, not a repetition.

What it is

The conclusion is the closing section of a piece. For nonfiction it restates the point in a fresh way and shows what it means; for a story it resolves the change the narrative has set up.

Why it matters

Readers remember endings. A strong conclusion leaves them with the point; a weak one — or one that drifts into new territory — makes the whole piece feel unfinished.

How to write it

Return to your main point. What did this piece establish or argue? State it once more, in a new way. Do not copy your introduction.

Show what it means. Why does the point matter? What should the reader take away or do next? This is the value of a conclusion.

Tie back to the opening. Referencing your introduction (a detail, a question, an image) closes the loop and feels satisfying.

Add a forward glance. For an argument, suggest the next step. For a story, show what changed. End with a sense of arrival.

Keep it short. A conclusion is rarely longer than a paragraph or two. If it is long, you probably introduced new material.

End on a strong final line. The last sentence is the note a reader leaves with. Make it clear and confident, not trailing off.

How to start it

You do not need a formula like "In conclusion." A natural transition ("The point matters because…", "What this means in practice is…") or a direct return to the idea works better and sounds less mechanical.

Common mistakes

Introducing a brand-new argument. Simply repeating the introduction word for word. Fading out with "In conclusion, to sum up, overall." Ending on a cliché or a shrug. Leaving the reader with nothing.

Practical tips

  • Write the conclusion after the body so it reflects what you actually built.
  • Cut any new idea that crept in; move it to the body or save it for another piece.
  • Read the last line alone — it should stand on its own.
