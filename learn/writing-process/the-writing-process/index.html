Writing process

The writing process

The repeatable ten-stage workflow that takes an idea to a published, finished piece.

Beginner Start here — assumes no prior knowledge.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Writing process guides

Writing is not one act; it is a sequence of separate acts. Trying to do them all at once is why drafts stall. Separate them and each stage becomes manageable.

What it is

The writing process is the standard workflow: idea → research → planning → outline → first draft → revision → editing → proofreading → final check → submission/publication.

Why it matters

Each stage has a different job. Drafting is about getting words down; revising is about improving them; editing is about fixing sentence-level issues; proofreading is about catching typos. If you try to do all four while you write, you slow yourself down and tire yourself out.

Step-by-step guide

1. Idea. Choose the specific thing you want to say. A clear idea beats a big vague topic.

2. Research. Gather what you need: facts, sources, notes, and examples. Do this before you plan, so the plan is based on real material.

3. Planning. Decide the shape and the main points. This is thinking on paper.

4. Outline. Arrange the points in order with a rough note for each section.

5. First draft. Write it quickly and roughly. Do not edit here; just produce.

6. Revision. Step back and improve the big picture: structure, argument, clarity, relevance. Move, cut, and add sections.

7. Editing. Fix the sentence-level craft: word choice, flow, repetition, conciseness, grammar.

8. Proofreading. Hunt for typos, punctuation errors, and inconsistencies. No new ideas here.

9. Final check. Read it as a reader would. Run a checklist. Confirm it answers the question, is complete, and is error-free.

10. Submission or publication. Send it through the right channel or publish it where it belongs.

Common mistakes

Editing while drafting. Polishing the first paragraph for an hour instead of finishing the draft. Treating proofreading as editing, so you fix typos but never revisit the structure. Rewriting the whole thing instead of fixing the parts.

Practical tips

  • Do not let one stage bleed into the next; when drafting, do not edit.
  • Give the draft a little time before revision so you see it fresh.
  • Revise big to small: structure first, then sentences, then typos.
  • Keep each stage short and separate so it does not become exhausting.
