Writing process

How to write under deadline pressure

What to do when the deadline is real, the draft is not, and you cannot afford to wait for the right mood.

Intermediate Assumes you can already draft and revise a piece.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Writing process guides

There is a specific kind of paralysis that only happens under deadline: the less time there is, the less able you are to start, because starting means confronting how little time there is. Every hour spent in that state makes the next hour worse.

The way out is not motivation. It is triage — deciding, deliberately, what this piece will not be.

Decide what it will not be

You cannot produce your best work in a quarter of the time, and pretending otherwise is what keeps you frozen. Ask instead: what is the minimum this piece must do to succeed?

An essay must answer the question with evidence. It does not need an elegant opening. A report must give findings and recommendations. It does not need a polished literature section. An article must have the reporting and the structure. It does not need the perfect first line.

Write down the two or three things that are non-negotiable. Everything else is now explicitly optional, and can be added if time remains. This single act does more for output under pressure than any productivity technique.

Work backwards from the deadline

Take the real deadline, subtract a buffer, and split what is left:

  • 50% drafting
  • 25% revising
  • 15% proofreading and formatting
  • 10% buffer — for the thing that will go wrong

People under pressure spend 90% on drafting and submit something unproofed, which is where the marks and the credibility actually go. An unedited draft reads as carelessness even when the thinking is good.

Write the times down. "Draft done by 3pm" is a decision; "get it done today" is a wish.

Draft badly, on purpose

Under deadline, the enemy is the sentence you rewrite four times before moving on. Give yourself permission to write the placeholder:

[something here about why the 2019 data is unreliable — check Adeyemi]

and keep going. You will write the linking sentence far faster once the surrounding material exists. Bracket every gap and move forward. Never stop to research mid-sentence; note it and continue.

Write the middle first. Introductions are the slowest paragraph to write and the easiest to write last, because by then you know what you are introducing.

Structure the time, not the willpower

  • Twenty-five-minute blocks with the phone in another room. Not for productivity mysticism — the physical distance is what works.
  • One block, one section. Name it before you start.
  • Stop mid-sentence when a block ends. Restarting is far easier when there is an obvious next word.
  • Do not reread from the top. Under pressure this becomes an avoidance ritual dressed as editing.
  • Sleep if there is a night available. Four hours of sleep then two hours of work beats six hours awake, on every measure including error rate. If there is no night available, protect the proofread rather than the draft.

The last hour

Do not write anything new. Do this instead:

  1. Check it answers the actual question or brief — reread the brief, properly, out loud.
  2. Check the structure holds: does each section do what its heading says?
  3. Fix the introduction and conclusion. These carry disproportionate weight with any reader.
  4. Run a spellcheck, then read the first and last sentence of every paragraph.
  5. Check the mechanics: name, word count, references, file format, file name.
  6. Submit with time to spare. Upload systems fail at the deadline, and "the portal crashed" is rarely accepted.

Common mistakes

Researching until there is no time to write — the most common failure by a wide margin. Polishing paragraph one while paragraph six does not exist. Adding scope under pressure. Skipping the proofread. Pulling an all-nighter and submitting work you cannot assess. Waiting to feel ready. Asking for an extension an hour before rather than two days before, when it might have been granted.

Practical tips

  • Set the timer before you decide whether you feel like it.
  • Keep a "gaps" list at the bottom of the document rather than fixing things inline.
  • If you are stuck on a section, write the sentence that says what the section will do, and move on.
  • Tell someone your intermediate deadline. External accountability is cheap and effective.
  • Afterwards, note what actually took the time. Under-estimating the same stage repeatedly is a solvable problem.
