Examples of writing

Example of a product description

A complete product description with the structure that sells without over-claiming.

Beginner Start here — assumes no prior knowledge.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Examples of writing guides

A product description has one job: help someone decide whether to buy. The example below is complete and realistic, followed by the structure that does the work without making wild claims.

The example product description

The Steady Pen — a pen that never runs out of ideas

Most pens die at the worst moment — mid-sentence, in a meeting, during a thought you cannot afford to lose. The Steady Pen is built for the ones who write in a hurry. Its refillable ink cartridge holds three times the ink of a standard pen, it writes smoothly even at speed, and it sits in your hand with a weight that feels like a real tool.

Whether you are signing contracts, sketching ideas, or filling a page with notes, the Steady Pen keeps up. It is the pen you reach for when losing the thought is not an option.

Why you'll like it

  • Three times the ink in a refillable cartridge — fewer interruptions.
  • Smooth, consistent line at any speed.
  • A balanced grip that is comfortable for long writing.
  • Made of a durable, lightweight metal that feels built to last.

Details

Refillable cartridge · Metal body · Available in black, blue and red ink · Writes for roughly a year of daily use · Refills sold separately.

Why this works

It opens on a problem, not the product. "Most pens die at the worst moment" names a frustration the reader recognises. That hooks them before they are told anything about the pen.

It translates features into benefits. The pen is not described as "3x ink" in a vacuum — the reader is told what that means: "fewer interruptions." For every feature, the description explains what it does for the buyer.

It uses an accessible structure. A short intro, a "Why you'll like it" list, and a details block. A reader can skim and get the gist.

It is honest, not over-claiming. It says what the pen does — refillable, comfortable, durable — without promising it will make you a better writer. Honesty builds trust.

It ends with concrete details the buyer actually needs: colours, refills, durability.

The structure to copy

  1. Open on a problem the buyer recognises.
  2. Introduce the product as the answer.
  3. List benefits — what each feature means for the buyer.
  4. Give the details — specs, options, and what is included.

How to adapt it

  • Start with the customer's problem, not your product.
  • Turn every feature into a benefit ("what does this do for them?").
  • Use a scannable list for the benefits.
  • Keep claims honest and specific.

Common mistakes

Listing features with no benefit. Over-claiming ("the best pen ever"). Jargon. A wall of text with no structure. Forgetting the practical details a buyer needs.
