How to structure a research paper

The standard sections of a research paper and what each one does.

Researched and written by BRYME Editorial Desk.

← All Academic writing guides

A research paper follows a standard structure, and that structure is a gift: it tells you what each section is for, so you never have to reinvent the shape. Learn the sections and you know how to build a paper.

The standard sections

Abstract. A short summary of the whole paper: the question, the method, the main results, and the conclusion. Many people read only this, so it must stand alone.

Introduction. The background, the gap, and your question or thesis. It tells the reader why the study matters and what it asks.

Methodology. How you did the study — who, what, how, and with what tools. The goal is reproducibility: a reader could repeat it.

Results. What you found, without interpretation. Use tables, figures, and plain statements of the data.

Discussion. What the results mean. Interpret them, compare them to prior work, note limitations, and say what they imply.

Conclusion. What the paper established and what it suggests for future work. No new results here.

References. Every source cited, in the required style.

How to build the structure

Start from the question, not the sections. What are you asking, and what would answer it? Let the question drive what goes in the results and discussion.

Write the abstract last. You cannot summarise what you have not written. Write it once the paper is done.

Use the introduction to set up the problem. Give context, state the gap ("prior work has not examined…"), and pose your question. A clear thesis makes the paper coherent.

Keep results and discussion separate. Results are the data; discussion is what it means. Mixing them makes a paper harder to follow and is a common review complaint.

Let citations do honest work. Cite what you drew on, and be honest about where your contribution sits and its limits.

How to keep it clear

Number your sections (1. Introduction, 2. Methodology, etc.) for easy navigation. Use headings and subheadings. Write plainly — a paper is not made harder by being clear. Signpost ("In this section, we…") so the reader knows where they are.

Common mistakes

Writing the abstract before the research. Burying the question. Mixing results and discussion. Never stating limitations. A references list that misses what you actually used. Padding to reach a length.

Practical tips

  • Use the outline generator to map the sections first.
  • Write the sections in order, but the abstract last.
  • State the question clearly in the introduction and return to it in the conclusion.
